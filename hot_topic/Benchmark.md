  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>

- **[TerraTransfer++: Demonstration-Free End-to-End Planner](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30319)**  `arXiv:2610.30319`  `cs.RO` `cs.AI` `cs.CV` `cs.LG`  
  _Zikang Xiong, Weixin Li, Zhouchonghao Wu, Akshay Rangesh, Saarth Bonde, Grantland Hall, et al._
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  End-to-end autonomous driving has achieved state-of-the-art performance on benchmarks and real-world deployments. Its standard training recipe, however, is expensive across all existing paradigms: imitation learning requires fleet-scale driving logs whose behavior distribution only thinly covers the safety-critical tail; closed-loop reinforcement learning in a photorealistic simulator, while offering diverse behaviors, is bottlenecked by sample-efficiency, per-step cost of rendering, and a forward pass through a large vision backbone. Self-play in vectorized simulators changes the economics: at millions of rollout steps per second, one can obtain a state distribution with natural diversity in collisions, near-misses, and recoveries that no driving log contains, and a highly reactive policy emerges from a basic driving-quality reward. Every target traces back to the self-play policy, so alignment never supervises against a logged trajectory: a paired dataset of (image, scene-state) frames suffices, with no need for the curated expert demonstrations that imitation pretraining is built on. From this one self-play teacher we obtain two end-to-end students: a closed-loop policy that outperforms every published imitation-based end-to-end method on the photorealistic 3D Gaussian splatting closed-loop benchmark HUGSim, and a planner that reaches 59.4 EPDMS on NAVSIM navhard, above every published method including DriveZero-Scale (57.1), and 95.5 PDMS on navtest, without human demonstrations.

- **[Scale and Selection: What Makes Automatic Harness Evolution Work for Visual-Interface Robot Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.39304)**  `arXiv:2609.39304`  `cs.RO` `cs.AI`  
  _Zhijie Wei, Ferris Tan, Jinghui Wang, Hao Zhou_
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  When an off-the-shelf coding agent is used directly as a robot policy, observing a browser-based 3D interface through screenshots and acting by posing a virtual target gripper through a few tools, the agent's harness, its prompts, tools, and control rules, largely determines success, and until now it has been written by hand. We show that this harness can be improved automatically by another coding agent, the optimizer agent, and report two findings about what makes it work. - **[Continuous-Time Motion Planning and Steering with Global Convergence](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30607)**  `arXiv:2610.30607`  `cs.RO`  
  _Mohsen Amiri, Sima Didari, Davoud Ataee Tarzanagh, Hankyu Moon, Yeongdae Kwon_
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Motion planning is central to robotics, and it is increasingly used to correct the motions proposed by learned planners and policies. In both uses, constraints must hold at every instant of the motion, not only at sampled times. To our knowledge, no algorithm provably converges to a globally optimal motion once a smoothness cost is combined with such constraints. We cast planning and steering as a single continuous-time program: robots avoid one another and static or moving obstacles, and a learned proposal is steered by keeping the motion close to it. The program is elementary to state, yet nonconvex. We propose GlobMP (Global Motion Planning), a gradient projection method over piecewise-polynomial curves, on which each constraint is one polynomial per piece that must stay nonnegative over the whole motion; it alternates cost reduction with feasibility restoration. Under mild assumptions, we show that, from a fine grid start, its cost approaches the global optimum as the number of polynomial pieces grows, with every constraint met to any chosen tolerance, or exactly on stable instances. We demonstrate its superior performance over strong baselines in real and synthetic experiments, including teams of up to $200$ robots, multi-robot planning on the Robotarium testbed and an extended benchmark, maze navigation, and steering tactile-reactive policies on a bimanual humanoid.
