- **[Self-Crystallizing Behavior Trees: Amortizing Foundation-Model Reasoning into Deterministic Control for Autonomous Drones](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.25455)**  `arXiv:2610.25455`  `cs.RO`  
  _Behnam Moradi_
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Classical robot control, Finite State Machines and Behavior Trees (BTs), is deterministic and inspectable but fixed at design time: it fails on situations its designers did not anticipate. Foundation models can synthesize new control logic at runtime, at a cost in latency and non-determinism hard to accept in safety-critical aerial systems. Existing LLM-driven approaches keep the model in the loop permanently and discard what it produces. We propose Self-Crystallizing Behavior Trees (SCBT), in which the foundation model is a compiler rather than a runtime. When a BT node fails, an LLM proposes a minimal, mission-agnostic subtree patch; CMA-ES tunes its parameters in simulation; a contextual bandit decides whether a stored patch or a new LLM call is worth its cost; and a three-stage admission pipeline (static check, invariant-monitored replays, an empirical margin over the unpatched tree) admits only patches that demonstrably recover. Admitted patches are stored in a fleet library and, once the LLM call rate decays, crystallized into a deterministic BT that no human wrote. We implement SCBT on Microsoft Project AirSim with a schema-constrained LLM pipeline (Gemini 3.1 Pro) and a lockstep simulator. Over three seeds of a 20-episode learning phase with wind-gust and battery failures, SCBT used $11\pm1.4$ LLM calls against 26 for whole-tree regeneration and 22 without a library, with 0.33 calls per episode in the second half. The crystallized trees, 28 and 33 nodes with two patches each, reached $97\pm2\%$ mission success with no learning running, against 12\% for the unpatched tree and 98\% for a hand-coded expert tree, and reproduce decision traces exactly when re-ticked offline. Two negative results: replay admission rejected useful wind recoveries by attributing wind-induced geofence excursions to the patch, and a static-only ablation generalized better to held-out failures.

- **[Refuse the Harm, Complete the Task via Graph-of-Rules Planning for LLM-Enabled Robots](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.24603)**  `arXiv:2610.24603`  `cs.RO`  
  _Ali Taheri, Alireza Taban, Qizhou Wang, Wanhua Li_
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly used in embodied AI for robotic manipulation and planning, but their deployment in physical systems exposes vulnerabilities such as jailbreak attacks that induce unsafe behavior. Existing inference-time defenses are mainly evaluated on short instructions containing a single hazard, whereas realistic tasks may interleave safe and unsafe sub-tasks, conceal hazardous steps, or combine individually safe actions into unsafe outcomes. Such compound instructions make it harder for a single safety decision to distinguish hazardous sub-tasks from legitimate ones, risking either overlooking the hazard or rejecting the whole instruction. We introduce safe task completion, a task that requires an LLM-enabled robot to refuse the harmful part of an instruction while completing its legitimate part. To address this problem, we propose RuleGraph, a multi-agent Graph-of-Rules planning framework that distributes safety enforcement across multiple reasoning and verification stages. Planning toward this goal is then organized as a graph of rule-specific nodes, where higher-level nodes inherit the union of their parents' rules, and candidate plans are progressively verified and aggregated until a single plan satisfies every relevant rule. In this way, RuleGraph reduces reliance on a single centralized safety decision. We evaluate RuleGraph on pure-attack and compound instructions across two benchmarks in two environments using Attack Success Rate (ASR) and Task Success Rate (TSR). RuleGraph reduces ASR to 0% on both benchmarks while achieving the highest TSR among all methods, reaching 98.57% on the RoboGuard benchmark and 84.54% on our long-horizon compound instructions.
