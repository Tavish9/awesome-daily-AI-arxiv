  _Ramy ElMallah, Marc Bishara, Hyegang Kim, Yuxi Zhang, Chongyu Zhu, Chi-Guhn Lee_
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Recent progress in embodied robotics is bringing robots closer to deployment in homes, as vision-language models (VLMs) are demonstrating significant advances in reasoning, making VLM-driven robots promising home assistant agents. These agents will often be introduced into homes that already have cameras, Internet of Things (IoT) devices, and residents with wearables. To fulfill their potential, they need to integrate with existing smart infrastructure and reason through ambiguous scenarios: a smoke alarm may come from a pan about to ignite, a pan already on fire, a resident cooking or a faulty sensor, and each calls for a different response. - **[WorldBridge: Bridging Video and Action for Task Generalization in World Action Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40748)**  `arXiv:2610.40748`  `cs.RO`  
  _Jun Cen, Yubo Guo, Hao Li, Liangkai Li, Yaoxu Lyu, Meng Wei, et al._
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Achieving the broad task generalization of embodied models in physical environments remains a central challenge in robot learning. We study this challenge in World Action Models (WAMs) through two complementary capabilities: video generalization, which predicts the visual evolution of new tasks, and action generalization, which translates these predictions into executable behavior through a video-to-action bridge. By varying video and action supervision for tasks outside an initial training set, we examine their respective contributions to task transfer. On RoboTwin 2.0, using only in-distribution video-action supervision yields a 25.07% success rate on out-of-distribution tasks. Introducing videos of these tasks without their action labels raises success to 55.90%, while adding just one action-labeled demonstration per task further improves it to 70.70%. These findings suggest that broader video coverage enables task transfer by leveraging existing inverse-dynamics capabilities, while even sparse action supervision further strengthens those capabilities. Guided by this perspective, we introduce WorldBridge, a WAM with separate video and action experts connected through structured attention and trained with joint flow matching. WorldBridge incorporates visual memory at two temporal scales and action forcing, alongside a unified data processing pipeline that standardizes and filters heterogeneous robot trajectories and egocentric hand-derived supervision. Across multiple manipulation benchmarks, WorldBridge achieves state-of-the-art performance among the compared methods, including 96.23% on RoboTwin 2.0 full data setting and 80.9% on clean to random setting, 82.8% on RoboCasa GR1 TableTop, and 57.6% on DOMINO Level-1. Our study highlights the complementary roles of video prediction and action grounding in developing robots that generalize across tasks.

  _Lukas Rustler, Matej Misar, Matej Hoffmann_
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  What is considered safe for a robot operator during physical human-robot collaboration (HRC) is specified in corresponding HRC standards (e.g., ISO 10218-2:2025). The regime that allows collisions between the moving robot and the operator, called Power and Force Limiting (PFL), restricts the permissible contact forces. Using the same fixed contact thresholds on the entire robot surface results unnecessary productivity losses, as the robot needs to stop even when impact forces are within limits. Here we present a framework that decides whether the robot should interrupt motion based on estimated collision force computed individually for different parts of the robot body and dynamically on the fly, based on the Effective Mass (EM) of each robot link. On a mock pick-and-place scenario with both transient and quasi-static collisions, we show an increase in productivity over 45% from using the standard approach. The method is applicable to any robot for which the EF can be calculated.

- **[Phantom Consensus: Measuring Social Reasoning in Multi-Agent LLM Decision-Making](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08971)**  `arXiv:2611.08971`  `cs.MA` `cs.AI` `cs.CL`  
  _Disha Sheshanarayana, Rajat Subhra Pal, Manjira Sinha, Tirthankar Dasgupta_
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Multi-agent LLM systems increasingly aggregate judgments across model instances, but reasoning about other agents can itself introduce failure. We study a setting in which LLMs shift their public decisions after observing ambiguous social signals while reporting inaccurate estimates of what other agents privately judged, even when the group's recorded private judgments do not support the apparent consensus. We call this failure phantom consensus and study it as a pluralistic-ignorance-style pattern in controlled LLM collectives. To measure this phenomenon, we introduce PHANTOM-Bench, a benchmark that separates isolated private judgments, public decisions after ambiguous social signals, elicited estimates of the group's private judgment, transparency-based recovery, and controls for neutral public text and explicit vote-following. Across nine closed- and open-weight models, ambiguous public signals consistently degrade decision quality and can turn correct private group judgments into incorrect public collective outcomes. Public accuracy drops by as much as 47.8 percentage points, and collective rationality failures reach 47.3% in the most susceptible setting. We observe substantial errors in reported group-private beliefs alongside public-stage failures, while control conditions reveal model-dependent differences in susceptibility to ambiguous and explicit peer signals. Revealing the agents' recorded private decisions repairs many failures without revealing the gold label, although recovery varies across models. Our results show that evaluating multi-agent LLM systems requires measuring not only final agreement, but whether that agreement reflects independently measured private judgments or an apparent consensus.

  _Mahdiyar Molahasani, Michael Greenspan, Ali Etemad_
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  We theoretically and empirically establish a previously unexplored connection between Long-Tailed Recognition (LTR) and Continual Learning (CL). Specifically, we show that training on a long-tailed dataset with imbalance factor $\mathrm{IF}$ drives model parameters into an $O(1/\sqrt{\mathrm{IF}})$ neighborhood of the dominant-class solution, under both uniform and exponentially decaying cardinality distributions. Building on this result, we establish an objective-level reformulation of LTR as a sequential learning problem and prove that the CL objective upper-bounds the balanced LTR loss up to a constant determined by the residual Head loss. In the foundation-model regime, CLTR with DualPrompt on a CLIP backbone outperforms standard adaptation baselines and is competitive with specialized methods using external semantic supervision. Our work bridges LTR and CL both theoretically and empirically, providing a principled approach for addressing long-tailed learning using standard CL strategies.

Unlike standard LLM abstention, which is usually evaluated as a single-turn answer-or-abstain decision, agentic abstention is a sequential decision problem: an agent can answer, abstain, or gather more information at each turn, and the need to abstain may only become clear after interacting with the environment.
