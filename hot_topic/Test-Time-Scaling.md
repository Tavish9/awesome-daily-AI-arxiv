- **[HexaShell: A Thin Harness for Exploring the Limits of VLM Agents as Robot Coding Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.23479)**  `arXiv:2610.23479`  `cs.RO`  
  _Hongcheng Gao, Alex Zhou, Jay Zhu, Zelin Zheng, Shijia Ge, Xiping Li, et al._
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Agents built on vision language models (VLMs) now compete with learned policies on robot manipulation leaderboards. We ask how far a coding agent can go when it controls a robot instead of a computer, and where it stops. Our system, HexaShell, keeps the harness thin: it only turns the agent's commands into robot motion. The agent drives a robot with two arms through one command line. It sees three RGB cameras and knows their calibration. After each failed attempt, the agent reads the log, finds the cause, and writes or fixes small programs called tools. In this way it keeps what it learns as code, as a coding agent does when it fixes a failing test. The tools may use only what the robot observes. They use triangulation to find where objects are, relative to each other and to the gripper. If two cameras see the same point, the point lies where the two camera rays meet. If a point lies on a known plane, such as the table or the top of a block, it lies where its ray meets that plane. When the head camera is not enough, the robot moves a wrist camera to get a second view. We build the tools on only ten layouts of one seed per task, then freeze them and test them on ten new layouts of the same seed. We use Codex with GPT-6-Astra at medium reasoning effort. On all 42 RoboDojo tasks, HexaShell reaches a success rate of 39.7% and a progress score of 48.0, averaged in the same way as the leaderboard. This is a new state of the art on RoboDojo. The limit is contact, not reasoning: pushing reaches 92.5% and memory tasks 65.0%, but tight insertion only 8.3%. When the agent fails, it usually sees the failure but cannot make the motion work. We open source the tools evolved so far atthis https URL.

- **[Can LLMs predict the outcomes of machine learning experiments?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22599)**  `arXiv:2610.22599`  `cs.LG` `cs.AI` `cs.CL`  
  _Ayush Bansal, Aldo Pareja, Sean Klein, Serge Didenko, Artem Lukoianov_
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Recent advances in AI make Recursive Self-Improvement (RSI) increasingly plausible. Large Language Models (LLMs) can already propose, select, and run Machine Learning (ML) experiments. However, the space of possible design choices in ML is vast. An LLM that proposes an experiment needs a reliable intuition of whether it is worth running given the timing constraints of long training runs. In this work, we measure such intuition in current frontier LLMs by evaluating how accurately they predict the impact of self-generated ML experiment ideas before these are put into training runs. To this end, we construct a dataset of 288 LLM-proposed ideas on eight MLE-bench tasks. Finally, the choice of the coding harness that actually implements the ideas has little effect on outcomes, and we find no evidence of self-preference. Higher reasoning settings and additional context lead to modest gains. Overall, our results show that current LLMs have useful but limited intuition about which ML ideas will work. We release our dataset to support further study.

- **[Process Reward Modeling with Reliability- and Conservatism-Aware Bayesian Belief](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.23223)**  `arXiv:2610.23223`  `cs.LG`  
  _Zhongjian Qiao, Zhixiang Zhou, Jiafei Lyu, Peisong Wang, Qiaosheng Zhang, Siyang Gao, et al._
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Process Reward Models (PRMs) play a central role in test-time scaling for large language model (LLM) reasoning. However, PRMs may provide inaccurate process rewards for test-time scaling, which we identify as arising from two sources of uncertainty: (1) \textit{PRM uncertainty} that arises from insufficient supervision data and imperfect PRM training; and (2) \textit{policy uncertainty} that results from the mismatch between the policy to collect supervision data and the policy for test-time scaling, leading to systematic reward overestimation. Previous studies fail to jointly consider both uncertainties. To bridge this gap, we propose BayesianPRM, which treats the process reward as a random variable within a reward hypothesis space and maintains a Bayesian belief over it. Since exact Bayesian inference over the continuous hypothesis space is generally intractable, we discretize the reward hypothesis space with a reward ensemble and introduce two complementary belief updates, each targeting one source of uncertainty: (1) to address PRM uncertainty, we introduce \textit{reliability-aware belief updating} that measures how well each ensemble member explains the supervision data and updates the belief with an amortized belief network optimized by the evidence lower bound (ELBO); and (2) to tackle policy uncertainty, we propose \textit{conservatism-aware belief calibration} which further reweights the belief toward lower rewards to suppress overestimation. These two updates yield a reliability- and conservatism-aware Bayesian belief, whose expectation determines the process reward. Experiments on multimodal reasoning benchmarks indicate that BayesianPRM consistently yields more accurate process reward estimates while enhancing test-time scaling performance.

  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Outcome-reward reinforcement learning such as GRPO gives every token of a search-agent rollout the same advantage, although the rollout exercises two capabilities: finding evidence and using it. We show that accuracy improves without grounding: Search-R1 raises accuracy on all seven QA benchmarks, yet on Bamboogle the evidence-supported share of correct answers falls from 84.0% to 42.9%.
