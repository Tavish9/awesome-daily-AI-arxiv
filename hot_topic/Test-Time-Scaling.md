  _Kun Chu, Xufeng Zhao, Jan-Gerrit Habekost, Cornelius Weber, Stefan Wermter_
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Bimanual robotic manipulation is versatile, yet its central challenge lies not only in executing dexterous motion with each hand, but also in coordinating the two arms for long-horizon manipulation under coupled spatial and temporal constraints. Prior work has largely focused on endowing robots with human-level manipulation skills, paying less attention to the structure of bimanual behaviour itself: many practical tasks are a coordinated composition of reusable unimanual skills and a small set of synchronous bimanual skills. This makes high-level spatio-temporal coordination, rather than skill acquisition alone, a key bottleneck for efficient bimanual execution. - **[Local Agreement Does Not Imply Global Consensus in LLM Panels](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.35885)**  `arXiv:2609.35885`  `cs.MA`  
  _Machiko Hirota, Akshara Nadayanur Sathis Kanna, Ujwal Kumar, Phan Xuan Tan_
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Local coordination in an LLM panel need not produce a common answer. We study 50-agent panels that update clock-time estimates after observing their neighbours. This circular response space separates agreement across the whole panel from agreement within neighbourhoods. Increasing the reasoning effort of gpt-5-mini reduces variation in local agreement, but all 20 higher-effort trials retain a one-twist configuration: neighbouring estimates remain close while the panel spans the clock. Degree-preserving rewiring instead produces global synchronisation in all 25 primary clock controls. A simple averaging model helps interpret the clock results but predicts their configurations poorly. The experiments support measuring global agreement and local structure separately, rather than inferring consensus from uniform local agreement.

- **[COSAC: Counterfactual Credit Assignment in Sequential Cooperative Teams](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.17693)**  `arXiv:2604.17693`  `cs.LG` `cs.AI` `cs.MA`  
  _Shripad V. Deshmukh, Jayakumar Subramanian, Raghavendra Addanki, Nikos Vlassis_
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  In cooperative teams where agents act in a fixed order and share a single team-level reward (multi-agent language systems, sequential robotic tasks), per-agent credit assignment is under-determined. Critic-based approaches scale poorly as the number of agents grows owing to the costly maintenance of joint/factored critic(s), whereas the existing critic-free alternatives have other issues: common credit across agents that couples every agent's signal to teammate noise, importance-sampling corrections for upstream-update staleness that incur variance exponential in team size, or per-agent counterfactual replay that isolates each agent's effect at the price of extra environment or reward calls. We propose COSAC, a critic-free policy gradient framework for sequential cooperative teams. COSAC fits an additive per-agent decomposition of the team reward by a single ridge regression on the rollout batch (giving each agent a learning signal decoupled from teammate noise), and computes each agent's counterfactual advantage from fictitious continuations of the current policy (policy forward passes that replace both importance-sampling reweighting and per-agent environment replay, at no extra environment or reward cost). The estimator instantiates the Sequential Aristocrat Utility (SeqAU), our extension of Wolpert and Tumer's (2001) aristocrat utility to sequential teams. We derive bias and conditional variance bounds for SeqAU credits: the direct-effect bound has no explicit team-size factor, while the indirect effect's reward-noise bound scales with the number of downstream agents. Sequential bandits test the analysis across team sizes up to K=16. In sequential multi-agent LLM fine-tuning, COSAC reduces training regret by 17% on consensus-based science reasoning relative to the best critic-free multi-agent baseline and by 61% on Python coding relative to MA-GRPO.

  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Reinforcement learning (RL) is central for aligning large language models (LLMs) with complex reasoning, yet the leading critic-free algorithm, Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), broadcasts a single sequence-level advantage uniformly across long Chain-of-Thought (CoT) generations and constrains exploration with an unbounded Kullback-Leibler (KL) penalty, which dilutes token-level supervision and induces gradient instability and mode-seeking conservatism.
