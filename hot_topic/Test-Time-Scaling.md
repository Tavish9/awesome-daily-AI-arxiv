- **[Tripwire: Robot Agents that Program Their Own Interrupts under Human Disturbance](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27182)**  `arXiv:2611.27182`  `cs.RO` `cs.CV`  
  _Sunli Chen, Yun Zong, Qinhong Zhou, Xiaowen Qiu, Jiaben Chen, Zeyuan Yang, et al._
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Agent harnesses are turn-based: the agent acts, waits for the result, then reasons. When the action invokes a pretrained robot policy, such as a vision-language-action (VLA) or world action model (WAM), the policy may react continuously to observations, but the agent cannot revisit its decision until the call returns. Meanwhile, a person may step into the robot's path or move the object it is about to grasp. Invoking the agent more often narrows this blind interval, but a reasoning model takes seconds per decision and most control steps need no new one. We propose TRIP, a training-free, event-driven harness in which the agent programs its own interrupts. With each call, the agent writes a tripwire: a lightweight, stateful Python program that runs alongside the policy, can query a compact VLM (for example, to locate a person and measure their distance), and decides whether the call continues or control returns to the agent. When it fires, the harness halts the policy and returns control with the reason; the agent then reassesses, adapts, and continues under a rewritten tripwire. We evaluate TRIP with three VLAs and one WAM on RoboHD, a benchmark of 168 RoboCasa365 scenes in which a simulated person disturbs the robot and any human-robot contact counts as failure. Compared with a turn-based harness using the same agent and tools, TRIP halves the rate of human contact with the strongest policy, raising safety from 61.3% to 81.3%, without sacrificing task success (38.7% vs. 31.0%). Real-robot experiments show that TRIP completes two manipulation tasks under human interference where the turn-based harness and the policy alone largely fail.

- **[Towards Understanding On-Policy Distillation through the Lens of Test-Time Scaling](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.11829)**  `arXiv:2608.11829`  `cs.LG` `cs.CL`  
  _Xinmu Ge, Zizhuo Zhang, Yu Huang, Jianing Zhu, Lin Yuan, Wanli Gu, et al._
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  On-policy distillation (OPD) has emerged as a promising post-training technique for enhancing LLM reasoning. Under the reverse KL objective, the idealized optimum of OPD aligns the student distribution with that of the teacher. When the teacher consistently outperforms the student, this naturally suggests that OPD should yield broad improvements over the pre-OPD student. However, do such improvements extend across the entire range of test-time sampling budgets? In this work, we revisit this expectation through the lens of test-time scaling by varying the sampling budget $K$ and evaluating performance with pass@$K$. Across multiple settings, we observe two distinct patterns: OPD can improve pass@$K$ at both small and large sampling budgets, but it can also improve small-budget performance while reducing large-budget pass@$K$. We show one condition that guarantees such a reversal and an idealized reverse KL counterexample where it occurs even when the teacher has higher accuracy on every problem. To choose between two candidate teachers at a target sampling budget, we propose the \textit{Teacher Advantage Score at $K$} (TAS@$K$), which can be computed before OPD training to predict which teacher will lead to a larger improvement in pass@$K$. Across three domains and thirteen benchmarks, the ordering predicted by TAS@$K$ agrees with the observed pass@$K$ improvements of the resulting OPD models in 83.6\% of experiments, providing a useful signal for teacher selection at the target pass@$K$.

- **[Self-Distilled Reasoner: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Large Language Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.18734)**  `arXiv:2601.18734`  `cs.LG` `cs.CL`  
  _Siyan Zhao, Zhihui Xie, Mengchen Liu, Jing Huang, Guan Pang, Feiyu Chen, et al._
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Knowledge distillation improves large language model (LLM) reasoning by compressing the knowledge of a teacher LLM to train smaller LLMs. On-policy distillation advances this approach by having the student sample its own trajectories while a teacher LLM provides dense token-level supervision, addressing the distribution mismatch between training and inference in off-policy distillation methods. However, on-policy distillation typically requires a separate, often larger, teacher LLM and does not explicitly leverage ground-truth solutions available in reasoning datasets. Inspired by the intuition that a sufficiently capable LLM can rationalize external privileged reasoning traces and teach its weaker self, we introduce On-Policy Self-Distillation (OPSD), a learning algorithm where a single LLM acts as both teacher and student with different contexts. The teacher policy conditions on privileged information (e.g., verified reasoning traces) while the student policy sees only the question; training minimizes the per-token divergence between these distributions over the student's own rollouts. We demonstrate the efficacy of our method on multiple mathematical reasoning benchmarks, achieving superior token efficiency compared to reinforcement learning methods and better performance over off-policy distillation methods. - **[Computation, Not Memory: Chain-of-Thought and State Tracking in LLM Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.25760)**  `arXiv:2610.25760`  `cs.LG` `cs.AI` `cs.CL`  
  _Pedram Mouseli, Massieh Moayedi_
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Language-model agents often reason before they act, and the value of monitoring that reasoning depends on how it relates to their actions. We ask how an agent's actions depend on its reasoning when the correct action depends on a changing state. In a small file-system task, background events announce which file's lock has been toggled but not its new value. The agent therefore has to reconstruct the current locks from its history before it can move files safely. With Qwen3-8B and Qwen3.5-9B, we separate two roles of reasoning: generating it before each action, and retaining earlier reasoning in the prompt. Most of the reduction in lock-violating commands comes from reasoning written at the moment of action. Retaining earlier reasoning adds little when the agent also reasons at each step, but it helps an agent that does not reason, and it helps in longer episodes. Retained notes have a limit: when a later event makes a note wrong, an agent without current reasoning responds little, while current reasoning makes the action follow the event. The benefit depends on what the reasoning says. Filler text, reasoning written for another history and an accurate table of the state do not reproduce it. When we give the agent reasoning written for a history that differs in one lock value, the agent acts on that reasoning even against its own history. For Qwen3-8B, this holds also when that value has since been toggled. Without reasoning, state readouts are less reliable after toggles, and Qwen3-8B's answers about the state favor the outdated value. The state answer and the action also depend on different parts of the prompt. In this setting, the benefit of reasoning for rule-following comes mainly from generating it at each step, where it makes the action respond to later events. The state that can be read out without reasoning is a limited guide to how the agent acts.

- **[Generalizing Test-time Compute-optimal Scaling as an Optimizable Graph](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.00086)**  `arXiv:2511.00086`  `cs.LG` `cs.AI` `cs.CL`

  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Test-Time Scaling (TTS) improves large language models (LLMs) by allocating additional computation during inference, typically through parallel, sequential, or hybrid scaling. However, prior studies often assume fixed collaboration architectures (e.g., topologies) and single-model usage, overlooking that optimal architectures and model combinations can vary across tasks. Therefore, we study the novel problem of searching for compute-optimal model combinations and architectures in TTS under a fixed budget. We formalize it as a multi-LLM collaboration graph, where nodes encode roles and LLM model assignments, and edges capture information flow. Experiments show that Agent-REINFORCE outperforms both traditional and LLM-based baselines in sample efficiency and search performance, and effectively identifies optimal graphs under joint objectives of accuracy and inference latency.

- **[THRIFTY: Cost-Constrained Reasoning-Data Synthesis for Small Language Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27436)**  `arXiv:2611.27436`  `cs.LG` `cs.AI`  
  _Aasheesh Singh, Vishal Vaddina_
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Small language models (SLMs) are increasingly attractive for efficient, specialized deployment, and are typically trained on reasoning trajectories distilled from a larger teacher LLM. Generating this synthetic data is a major cost of the pipeline, especially when the teacher is a frontier model. Existing collection methods, however, are largely cost-unaware, or at best spread a fixed sampling budget uniformly across queries. Such allocation ignores the wide variation in problem difficulty and the strategic over- or under-sampling it invites. Thus, we propose THRIFTY, a Thompson-sampling bandit controller that spends a fixed generation-token budget to collect diverse correct reasoning trajectories, casting the task as a bandits-with-knapsacks problem with a novel diversity-aware objective. It tracks each problem's success probability with an online Beta posterior and spends each token where it expects the most new distinct correct solutions. Because repeated solutions add progressively less value, THRIFTY adaptively stops resampling saturated problems, concentrates budget on under-covered ones, and retires the unsolvable at the budget's opportunity cost. On math (AIME, MATH-500) and code (LiveCodeBench) benchmarks, THRIFTY gathers up to 22% more diverse correct trajectories per generation token than both the standard uniform-sampling recipe and the strongest adaptive baseline, with the largest gains in the scarce-budget and hard-problem regimes.

- **[Benchmarks Are Surprisingly Compaction-Friendly](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31778)**  `arXiv:2610.31778`  `cs.LG` `cs.AI`  
  _Jeanne Salle, Hardik Bhatnagar, Matthias Bethge, Sahar Abdelnabi, Maksym Andriushchenko_
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Long horizon agentic tasks are now pushing LLM agents past their context window. To continue the task, the agent scaffold has to free up the context window, a process known as context compaction. Compaction is generally lossy by design: agents can silently lose task constraints, instructions, or their reasoning behind earlier decisions. Although compaction is a standard component of coding agent scaffolds, it has not been studied systematically with frontier models on realistic, long horizon agentic tasks. Previous work evaluate smaller open weight models on toy or synthetic trajectories. We fix the agent scaffold (OpenCode), implement five compaction strategies inside it, and evaluate three frontier models on three long horizon benchmarks (PaperBench, ProgramBench, PostTrainBench). For the two 1M context models, the compaction threshold does not measurably affect the task performance, whereas GPT-5.3-Codex, the model with the smallest context window, performs worse at lower thresholds on PaperBench and, less clearly, on PostTrainBench. No compaction method outperforms default self-summarization, and methods that discard more content cost some models up to 20 points while leaving other models the same. Compaction can, however, silently remove task constraints: on ProgramBench, the first compaction summary already drops about one third of the task's anticheating rules on average, and this rises monotonically with the number of compactions. On real user-agent sessions from SWE-chat, instructions about how the agent should work and what it should not do are dropped around 6 times more than specifications of what to build. We argue that current benchmarks are compaction-friendly: i.e., the objective is fixed and the agent's progress stays on disk, so most of what compaction discards can be recovered, except constraints that don't exist anywhere else.
