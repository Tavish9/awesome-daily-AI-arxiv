- **[Finetuning VLAs for Dexterous Manipulation: The Right Checkpoint Is All You Need](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.23676)**  `arXiv:2611.23676`  `cs.RO` `cs.LG`  
  _Artur Kuramshin, Charlotte Cloutier, Michael Przystupa, Kirsty Ellis, Mohammad S. Akhter, Glen Berseth_
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Vision-Language-Action models have demonstrated strong manipulation capabilities. A limitation of VLA models is that most manipulation datasets focus on parallel-jaw gripper systems, making transfer to multi-finger hands challenging, particularly as teleoperation datasets are harder to collect with multi-fingered robots. In this work, we study how to best adapt VLAs to dexterous hands by pairing supervised fine-tuning (SFT) with diffusion-steering reinforcement learning (DSRL). Using $\pi_{0.5}$ and a LEAP hand, we evaluate five fine-tuning recipes across two simulation tasks using only a handful of demonstrations. Our empirical analysis yields three key insights: (1) SFT success is poorly correlated with post-DSRL success, meaning the best SFT checkpoint is rarely the most effective to steer; (2) freezing the vision-language model (VLM) and tuning only the action expert produces the most steerable policy for both training and out-of-distribution placements; and (3) low-rank adaptation (LoRA) is the least steerable approach. Consequently, we recommend an adaptation recipe that freezes the VLM, fully trains the action expert, and eschews SFT rollout success as a checkpoint selection metric.

- **[CriticHack: Evaluating Visual Rewards Under Robot Policy Optimization](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02527)**  `arXiv:2610.02527`  `cs.RO` `cs.AI` `cs.LG`  
  _Jiaxuan Luo, Xingguo Xu, Shanshan Wang, Yuhan Zhou, Zhen Zhang_
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Learned visual reward models score a robot's execution from camera images and an instruction, and are increasingly used to fine-tune robot policies. Two checks decide whether to trust one: the reward model separates successes from failures, and task success rises during training. We show that both checks can pass while the policy learns to act on the wrong object. When a diffusion policy is fine-tuned against the reward model Robometer to open a drawer, task success rises by 10.2 percentage points. Wrong-object failures, which open a different drawer, rise by 10.9 points, whereas the same training on the simulator's task-completion signal raises success alone. A second reward model, RoboReward-8B, behaves the same way, and the effect reappears on a physical robot. This happens because of how the reward scores the policy's own failures. Most failures make little progress and score low, while a wrong-object failure looks like progress and scores well above them. Optimization shifts a policy toward outcomes that score above the executions they compete with, the executions they can take the place of. It therefore turns low-scoring failures into both successes and wrong-object failures, even when the reward model scores successes higher. This rule needs only executions of the initial policy, so it can be tested before training. On a stacking task we predicted which of three object placements Robometer would exploit, and training raised wrong-object stacks from 20% to 69% in that placement and in neither of the others. The same rule gives a check to run before optimization and a repair that needs no labels: scoring each execution again on frames re-rendered with the objects the instruction does not name put back removes the amplification on both tasks and on the robot.

- **[RecallFlow: Episodic Memory as the Source Distribution of Generative Robot Policies](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.23731)**  `arXiv:2611.23731`  `cs.RO`  
  _Xuji Zhang, Qilin Zhong, Zichen Zhang, Wei Zhai, Jian Di, Kun Li, et al._
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Diffusion and flow-matching policies have become a standard choice for visuomotor imitation learning, owing to their ability to model multimodal action distributions. In memory-dependent manipulation, the robot must act on information that is no longer visible. Existing methods feed the observation history to the policy as an additional condition, while action generation still starts from Gaussian noise. However, the policy must then pick out the relevant target from a long and redundant history, leading to spurious correlations and unreliable decisions as the memory load grows. Additionally, the initial noise carries no information about what the robot has seen, leaving generation without guidance toward the remembered target. To address these issues, we propose RecallFlow, a flow-policy framework that builds the source distribution from episodic memory. Recall-Relocalization relates the memory to the current view through a few recall slots, so that the source reflects where the remembered targets are now. Recall Anchors then turns each slot into a Gaussian component of the source, enabling generation to start close to the intended action instead of from noise. The transport network then completes the action from the current observation alone, keeping inference nearly as cheap as a memoryless policy. We further build a suite of memory-dependent tasks grouped by memory load and compare all policies under a matched protocol. Extensive experiments show that RecallFlow achieves a state-of-the-art average success rate of 80.4% on the public MIKASA-Robo memory benchmark, and outperforms the strongest baseline by 20.3 points on our suite and by 18.8 points on four real-robot tasks.

Clustering-based sparse attention clusters both queries and keys and lets all queries in a cluster share one selection of key clusters.
