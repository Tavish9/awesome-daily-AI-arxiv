- **[Vision-Language Models Are Joint Denoisers in Robotic Action Generation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.01215)**  `arXiv:2611.01215`  `cs.RO` `cs.AI` `cs.CV`  
  _Xicheng Gong, Wenxuan Song, Chengze Liu, Qiwei Li, Qize Yu, Jiaolong Yang, et al._
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Diffusion-based vision-language-action (VLA) models cascade pretrained vision-language models (VLMs) with diffusion action experts, but typically restrict the VLM to providing static conditioning. This separation limits how much pretrained perception and reasoning can guide action generation, especially in out-of-distribution settings. We introduce JADE, a Joint Action DEnoiser that enables the VLM and action expert to jointly refine actions. Our key insight is that structured action latents allow the VLM to read the current noisy action together with visual observations and language instructions. At every denoising step, the VLM contextualizes the noisy action and guides the action expert through gated cross-attention, so that its guidance evolves with the action being generated. We further supervise the VLM to recover clean action latents from noisy ones, which gives it a direct action-learning signal and couples perception with control. Additionally, joint denoising enables JADE to generate actions with fewer sampling steps, helping offset the additional cost of VLM participation. An adaptive step-size schedule further balances task performance and inference latency. On RoboDojo, JADE achieves an average success rate of 12.67%, the highest among the evaluated $\pi_{0.5}$-based methods, and is competitive with recent VLA and WAM foundation models pretrained at scale. It also improves performance on RoboTwin 2.0 and raises the average success rate across four real-world bimanual tasks by 20.8 percentage points compared with $\pi_{0.5}$.

- **[Does Validation Loss Predict Real-Robot Success? Evidence from Four Imitation-Learning Policies on a Low-Cost Arm](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.00283)**  `arXiv:2611.00283`  `cs.RO`  
  _Simon Cowling_
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Held-out validation loss is the standard offline signal for choosing checkpoints and comparing models, but its relation to closed-loop success on a real robot is rarely measured. We measure it for ACT, Diffusion Policy, SmolVLA and $\pi_{0.5}$ (action expert only), trained on nested subsets of 12 to 200 demonstrations of a pick-and-place task on an SO-101 arm. We score 82 checkpoints on 25 held-out demonstrations, with each policy's training loss and with the error of the actions it outputs, and compare the scores with 930 bench rollouts. We also score all 700 intermediate checkpoints of 70 training runs, and recover the ball positions of every training, held-out and bench episode from the front camera. Within each policy, hold-out action error correlates with bench success (Spearman $\rho$ from -0.23 to -0.88), but no more strongly than the number of training episodes alone, and not at all among seeds trained on the same data. Across policies the ranking is inverted ($\rho$ = +0.90 at 200 episodes): the policy with the lowest hold-out error succeeded in 3 of 110 rollouts. The most common failure, the arm never leaving its rest pose, ended 96% of rollouts for Diffusion Policy trained on untrimmed demonstrations and 47% for SmolVLA, and cannot occur in a hold-out set made of demonstrations. Among rollouts in which the arm moved, SmolVLA's hold-out action error predicts success well ($\rho$ = -0.81). For the diffusion and flow-matching policies, hold-out loss rises two- to five-fold during training while hold-out action error falls by up to 29%. Held-out metrics are a sound diagnostic of learning but no substitute for evaluation on the robot, and we recommend reporting initiation failure and success among started rollouts as separate bench outcomes.
