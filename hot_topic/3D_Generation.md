  _Chenxi Han, Ruby, Epoch, Orion, Luke, Dusk, et al._
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  When humans learn to manipulate an unfamiliar object, they may already be able to anticipate how its state will change while still being unable to control it skillfully. This gap between prediction and control motivates us to study how the priors acquired by video generation models can be used effectively by an action policy. We present ZERITH V2.5, a video-action model (VAM) in which the action policy reads intermediate features of a video generation model, and use it to study, under fixed demonstrations and a fixed total number of updates, what kind of predictive training improves subsequent action learning.We find that the way video predictive training is supervised affects subsequent action learning. In our experiments, adapting at many noise levels yields a large gain, while adapting only at the noise level used at deployment yields none. Weight-transfer experiments further show that the gain stays with the adapted weights when they remain in the video generation model and disappears when they are used only to initialize the action policy. Initial loss and gradient measurements at the start of joint training indicate that target-domain predictive training gives the video model a better starting state, making subsequent joint learning of prediction and action easier. Under matched data and training budgets, predictive adaptation improves control. We evaluate the complete policy on LIBERO, LIBERO-Plus, Meta-World MT50, and real-robot tasks.

- **[SharQ: Bridging Activation Sparsity and FP4 Quantization for LLM Inference](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.26587)**  `arXiv:2606.26587`  `cs.LG` `cs.AI`  
  _Haoqian Meng, Yilun Luo, Yafei Zhao, Wenyuan Liu, Huaqing Zheng, Xindian Ma, et al._
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Low-bit floating-point formats and semi-structured sparsity are increasingly supported by modern accelerators, yet combining them for LLM activation compression remains challenging: activations contain input-dependent outliers that dominate block scales in FP4 quantization, and directly applying N:M sparsity masks discards moderate values, coupling sparsification loss with quantization error. We introduce SharQ, a training-free inference method that bridges activation sparsity and FP4 quantization through an online sparse--dense decomposition. For each activation tensor, SharQ generates an input-adaptive N:M mask to extract an outlier-dominated sparse backbone, quantizes it to FP4, and defines a dense residual relative to the quantized sparse backbone rather than the unquantized sparse values. A sparse FP4 GEMM processes the backbone while a dense FP4 GEMM compensates for both mask-induced activation loss and sparse-path quantization error. The two paths share a single FP4 weight payload with path-specific scale views, and a fused preparation kernel absorbs mask generation, residual construction, and layer normalization into one operator. SharQ requires no calibration data, retraining, or model-specific tuning. Evaluated on Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen2.5-7B, Qwen3-30B-A3B, and Qwen3-VL-8B, SharQ recovers 43--63% of the NVFP4-to-FP16 accuracy gap across language and vision-language tasks, and generalizes across NVFP4, HiF4, and MXFP4 formats. On an RTX 5090, SharQ delivers 2.2--2.4$\times$ latency reduction over FP16 and 1.2--1.4$\times$ throughput improvement over FP8 in language model serving, and up to 1.58$\times$ speedup on Wan2.2-T2V-A14B video generation when combined with SageAttention.   _Xin Wang, Wenxuan Liu, Tongtong Feng, Wenwu Zhu_
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Large-scale video generation models are increasingly described as world models because they can learn rich spatiotemporal regularities from visual data. We argue that an ideal world model should benefit in a self-evolving generative character. As such, a self-evolving world model can be realized via a generative model conditioned by various constraints, whose future predictions are refined through feedback. However, traditional visually plausible predictions alone are not enough to establish whether an imagined future is physically actionable for a particular embodied agent, failing to provide informative feedback from environments for self-evolving improvement. To realize self-evolving world models, this article proposes the concept of autonomous video generation, which is evaluated through counterfactual controllability, i.e., the ability to i) generate intervention-conditioned futures, ii) bind these future frames to embodiment constraints, iii) verify them under distribution shifts, and iv) distil surviving branches into compact variables for decision-making. We formalize a four-stage closed-loop optimization of Generation, Binding, Verification and Distillation, together with four corresponding evaluation metrics: novelty, consistency, out-of-distribution (OOD) and efficiency. We further discuss two examples, i.e., drones and manipulators, as early embodied testbeds where wind, sensing limits, actuation delay, contact dynamics and recovery constraints can be systematically perturbed and verified. The central claim is that the framework of autonomous video generation for self-evolving world models should not be judged by video fidelity alone, but by whether the generated frames improve valid action under counterfactual interventions and various embodiment constraints.

- **[PhysResponse: Probing the Physics of Video Generation with Controlled Interventions](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.24419)**  `arXiv:2611.24419`  `cs.CV`  
  _Yuhan Li, Shuran Zhang, Haixin Tian, Baonuo Rao, Weipeng Li, Yujia Hou, et al._
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Video generation models are increasingly viewed as world simulators, which requires not only plausible motion but dynamics that respond correctly to specified physical parameters. Existing models, datasets and benchmarks, however, largely target physical plausibility, leaving this physical parameter responsiveness insufficiently explored. We introduce PhysResponse, a benchmark that measures it at two levels: scene-conditioned simulator inversion recovers the effective gravity, friction and initial speed of generated motion, and event evaluation tests outcomes near and across critical transitions. PhysResponse provides a foundation for world simulators that follow physical conditions, not just appearances.
