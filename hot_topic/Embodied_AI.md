- **[Cairn: Object-Centric 3D Trajectory Priors for Steering Frozen VLAs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34914)**  `arXiv:2611.34914`  `cs.RO` `cs.CV`  
  _Agelos Kratimenos, Kostas Daniilidis, Evangelos Chatzipantazis_
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Vision-language-action models (VLAs) are capable low-level controllers, but they tie where objects go to the layouts they were trained on: when objects are moved or obstacles block the way, they carry them to the wrong place or into collisions. Fine-tuning on new layouts needs robot data for each one and erodes skills the model already has. We introduce Cairn, which keeps the VLA frozen and steers it at test time along learned 3D trajectories of the object in its gripper. A small prior reads point clouds of the scene and the held object, along with its pose when grasped and the instruction, and samples SE(3) trajectories up to its release that lead it around obstacles and turn it to fit through gaps. The prior and the VLA form the two levels of a hierarchical planner in which feedback runs both ways: the VLA's intended motion chooses and reshapes the trajectories, and the chosen one guides the VLA's denoising. Its predicted actions, mapped to the held object's poses through the grasp, are pulled toward the next waypoint, while the VLA's weights stay untouched. Across 1,941 held-out LIBERO scenes with displaced objects and obstacles, Cairn raises the success rate of a frozen {\pi}0.5 from 24.5% to 95.1%, above that of a {\pi}0.5 fine-tuned on our training layouts, without hurting standard LIBERO. On the third-party LIBERO-PRO benchmark, it raises success from 27.3% to 38.0%, and from 54.4% to 81.1% on the episodes it steers. On a real Franka, with a prior trained purely in simulation, it guides the frozen DROID {\pi}0.5 around an obstacle and to a placement at the limit of the arm's reach, where it otherwise fails. All code, models and data will be made public.

- **[TacInno: Teaching Vision-Language-Action Models the Contact Dynamics Beyond Vision and Action](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26405)**  `arXiv:2611.26405`  `cs.RO` `cs.CV`

  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have recently been equipped with tactile sensing to perceive visually subtle contact events in contact-rich manipulation. However, adding tactile input yields inconsistent gains, and some visuo-tactile VLA models rely little on tactile input, because much of the tactile change, such as the gel deformation during gripper closure, is predictable from visual observations and executed actions. Together, these results demonstrate the effectiveness of tactile innovation as a supervision signal for contact-rich manipulation.

- **[Best-of-$N$ Test-Time Steering Can Lead to Reward Hacking in Vision-Language-Action Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33398)**  `arXiv:2611.33398`  `cs.RO` `cs.AI` `cs.LG`  
  _Vanshaj Khattar, Jing Liu, Toshiaki Koike-Akino, Ming Jin, Ye Wang_
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Vision-language-action (VLA) models have shown promise for general-purpose robotic manipulation, but adapting to changing environmental conditions still remains challenging. Recent test-time steering methods have shown promise in enabling adaptive VLA policies by utilizing additional computation during deployment. In this paper, we consider best-of-$N$ test-time steering for VLA models, where multiple action candidates are sampled from a fixed policy and a verifier is used to select the best one during deployment. We show that increasing $N$ does not always improve performance when the verifier is inaccurate, and can favor candidates whose scores exploit prediction errors, a form of reward hacking. We introduce controlled errors in a MuJoCo trajectory verifier and show that as $N$ increases, selected candidate rewards diverge from the accurate verifier rewards, leading to reward hacking. We also show that verifier calibration and softmax-based candidate selection can mitigate reward hacking in some settings.

  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Robots should adapt to unfamiliar environments and acquire new skills through interaction, then execute those skills efficiently with practice. Vision-language-action policies provide efficient control, but often need additional human demonstrations to handle unfamiliar settings; reasoning agents can discover solutions using broad prior knowledge and robot-control APIs, but repeated deliberation and repair are costly. Inspired by human learning through reasoning and practice, we propose RoboEvo$^2$, a framework for recursive harness-policy co-evolution. - **[RoboAda: Adapting Frozen Robot Policies at Test Time to Physics They Cannot See](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.30209)**  `arXiv:2611.30209`  `cs.RO` `cs.AI`  
  _Yunbei Zhang, Zijian Jin, Shuaicheng Niu, Baian Chen, Weirui Ye, Jihun Hamm, et al._
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Pretrained robot policies decide what to do from camera images, yet some of the physics that decides whether their plans succeed is not visible. An object may look light but be 100 times heavier, and a weakened joint or a lagging controller leaves no trace in the image. When the physics at deployment differs from training, the plan is often still right but is executed as if nothing had changed. Training on such variations in advance cannot handle unseen changes, while adapting once deployed either changes the plan, leaving the miscalibrated commands in place, or updates a robot model by gradient steps that do not know how reliable they are and can make the robot worse even when nothing has changed. We propose RoboAda, which keeps the policy and its plan unchanged and corrects the commands that carry it out by inferring at test time how the robot turns commands into motion. The key observation is that the robot's own motion reveals the change: after each action chunk, an inverse-dynamics model of the original robot converts the observed motion into the commands it would have needed, yielding free labels at every step. Competing hypotheses of how the robot responds, including that nothing has changed, are weighted by how well they predict these labels, and the next chunk is sent with the commands that best realize the plan under them. RoboAda needs no reward, no gradient step and no data from the changed robot. We evaluate RoboAda with five frozen policies on LIBERO, two world action models and three vision-language-action models. Under eleven physical shifts, it more than doubles their average success, from 27.8% to 62.9%. It beats the best of seven test-time adaptation methods by 17.3 points on Cosmos Policy, and largely preserves the policies' success when nothing has changed. On bimanual RoboTwin 2.0 in joint space, it raises success under joint-target offsets from 24.9% to 78.8%.
