- **[Tripwire: Robot Agents that Program Their Own Interrupts under Human Disturbance](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27182)**  `arXiv:2611.27182`  `cs.RO` `cs.CV`  
  _Sunli Chen, Yun Zong, Qinhong Zhou, Xiaowen Qiu, Jiaben Chen, Zeyuan Yang, et al._
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Agent harnesses are turn-based: the agent acts, waits for the result, then reasons. When the action invokes a pretrained robot policy, such as a vision-language-action (VLA) or world action model (WAM), the policy may react continuously to observations, but the agent cannot revisit its decision until the call returns. Meanwhile, a person may step into the robot's path or move the object it is about to grasp. Invoking the agent more often narrows this blind interval, but a reasoning model takes seconds per decision and most control steps need no new one. We propose TRIP, a training-free, event-driven harness in which the agent programs its own interrupts. With each call, the agent writes a tripwire: a lightweight, stateful Python program that runs alongside the policy, can query a compact VLM (for example, to locate a person and measure their distance), and decides whether the call continues or control returns to the agent. When it fires, the harness halts the policy and returns control with the reason; the agent then reassesses, adapts, and continues under a rewritten tripwire. We evaluate TRIP with three VLAs and one WAM on RoboHD, a benchmark of 168 RoboCasa365 scenes in which a simulated person disturbs the robot and any human-robot contact counts as failure. Compared with a turn-based harness using the same agent and tools, TRIP halves the rate of human contact with the strongest policy, raising safety from 61.3% to 81.3%, without sacrificing task success (38.7% vs. 31.0%). Real-robot experiments show that TRIP completes two manipulation tasks under human interference where the turn-based harness and the policy alone largely fail.

  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>

The Actor interacts with the environment through a robot-control interface, while the Reflector converts execution evidence into reusable operational guidance. - **[An Autonomous Adaptive Learning System for Robotic Grasping in the Circular Factory](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27782)**  `arXiv:2611.27782`  `cs.RO`  
  _Yitian Shi, Rosa Wolf, Rania Rayyes_
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Linear manufacturing typically operates in highly structured settings with standardized objects, allowing robots to execute pre-programmed motions based on precise models. In contrast, circular manufacturing involves dynamic and less controlled environments, where product components can vary due to appearance and physical changes across different product generations. In this context, uncertainty arises from both the unknown or partially observed states of components (e.g., damaged surfaces, altered shapes) and the unstructured disassembly environment, often characterized by heavy clutter, occlusions, and limited sensing viewpoints. This demands a flexible and adaptable robotic grasping system that can cope with unexpected object changes. To address these challenges, this paper proposes an autonomous grasp learning framework designed for highly uncertain and variable conditions. The framework explicitly distinguishes between uncertainty due to noisy or incomplete sensory observations (aleatoric uncertainty) and uncertainty due to limited knowledge and distribution shift (epistemic uncertainty). Moreover, the framework employs an active perception strategy to reduce visual ambiguity: semantic and spatial scene understanding is achieved using vision language models (VLMs), while view planning enables the robot to adjust its viewpoint to improve observability. In addition, the approach leverages large-scale human demonstration data to learn interaction patterns, which are then transferred to robotic end-effectors, enabling robust manipulation in these variable settings. Quantitative validation, including the utility of active vision on disassembled components in practice, is expected to form an important part of future work.

  _Kangyi Wu, Pengna Li, Yucheng Wang, Yance Fang, Xihan Ma, Zhizhong Su, et al._
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Vision-Language Navigation(VLN) requires an embodied agent to follow language instructions through unseen 3D environments, where the target spaces often lie beyond the current observation. Navigation world models address this challenge by predicting future views either as pixels or latent representations, providing valuable look-ahead information for action decision. Both paradigms define the future primarily through what the agent may observe, raising a fundamental question: is predicting future views the ultimate form to represent the future for VLN? Cognitive studies show that the human hippocampus prospectively represents both future goals and intervening locations along planned routes. Inspired by this predictive-map view, we organize the navigation-relevant future around goal-directed spatial states and route structure. We instantiate this perspective with two complementary abstractions: a short-horizon waypoint that specifies the next local state to reach, and a mid-horizon corridor that captures the spatial support of the upcoming route. Based on this design, we propose FS-Nav, a future-state world modeling framework that predicts waypoint-corridor states from spatial memory and language instructions, and communicates them to a VLM navigator through top-down and first-person interfaces. Experiments on standard VLN benchmarks demonstrate that FS-Nav achieves state-of-the-art performance. This highlights navigation-relevant future-state modeling as a promising direction for representing the future in VLN.

  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>

- **[What Does a Zoom Actually Add? Decomposing Fine-Grained Perception Failures in Vision-Language Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26904)**  `arXiv:2611.26904`  `cs.CV` `cs.CL`  
  _Zhixuan Ge, Anqi Li, Kexin Geng, Wei Qiu_
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Zooming into a region often turns a wrong answer from a vision-language model (VLM) into a right one, and a growing literature takes this as evidence that the model lacked the pixels. But a zoom changes several things at once. It removes the surrounding context, re-tokenizes, at a higher density, pixels the model has already seen, and, if the first pass was downsampled, exposes detail the model never received. We separate these factors for each failure. Holding the first pass fixed, we vary only the second look and assign every failure to the cheapest one that fixes it: reusing cached tokens, isolating the region, re-tokenizing the seen pixels, or acquiring new ones. The study covers 2,753 zoom-rescued failures of Qwen2.5-VL, Qwen3-VL and InternVL3 on V*, ZoomBench and TextVQA, at first-pass budgets from 17x fewer pixels than the source to native resolution. Second, the first-pass budget decides how often new pixels are needed: depending on the benchmark, new pixels are required for 23-50% of the rescues at 17x and for 12-23% at 4x. The missing-pixel reading and the accounts that dispute it thus hold at different budgets. Third, the useful magnification is large and differs between objects and text: seen pixels fix more failures up to 6-12x their first-pass size per side on objects and saturate by 4x on text, and even native pixels gain from being rendered beyond native resolution. Allowing any magnification, 68-94% of the failures at 17x need no new pixels. These models more often need a better rendering of evidence they already have than new pixels, which motivates re-tokenizing a region before acquiring it.
