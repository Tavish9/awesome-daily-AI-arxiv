- **[QK-Bench: Benchmarking Quantitative Kinematic Understanding for Dynamic Spatial Intelligence](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33991)**  `arXiv:2611.33991`  `cs.RO` `cs.CV`  
  _Lennox Wei, Jiaqi Yao, Shixin Sun, Yuanzheng Zhang, Jianhua Han, Meng Tian, et al._
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Spatial intelligence concerns both scene structure and its temporal evolution. Yet a semantically correct description can conceal a quantitative error: recognizing that objects are approaching does not determine their closing speed or whether they will meet. Such errors are critical in collision avoidance, trajectory tracking, and reactive grasping, where actions depend on motion magnitude, timing, and relative geometry. We study quantitative kinematic understanding in multimodal large language models (MLLMs): resolving motion magnitude, rate, reference-frame dependence, and future spatial evolution from visual evidence. We introduce the Quantitative Kinematics Benchmark (QK-Bench), comprising 2,341 multiple-choice QA pairs from 379 videos and 17 tasks spanning Object-Centric Kinematics, Camera Ego-Motion Kinematics, and Multi-Object Kinematic Relations. Hidden geometric and temporal signals support trajectory construction and filtering, while fixed kinematic rules map physical quantities to verifiable answers, including categorical labels. Trajectory and question validation ensure target reliability and sufficient visual support in the sampled frames. All models receive a unified monocular RGB input. Evaluations of proprietary, open-source, and spatial MLLMs reveal substantial limitations: the strongest model achieves only 41.2\% overall accuracy, and spatial MLLMs show no consistent advantage. These findings indicate that current advances in spatial intelligence do not reliably transfer to quantitative kinematic understanding.
  </details>

- **[Greedy Decoding Hides Good Plans: Sample-and-Verify Planning for Vision-Language Driving Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33621)**  `arXiv:2611.33621`  `cs.RO` `cs.CV`  
  _Zhipeng Bao, Wenjie Zhao, Yiping Li, Haohua Que, Qianwen Li_
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Vision-language models (VLMs) are increasingly adopted for end-to-end driving planning, yet their performance is typically evaluated based on a single greedily decoded trajectory. However, greedy decoding may overlook better trajectories within the model's output distribution. Experiments on WOD-E2E and NAVSIM reveal a consistent performance gap between greedily decoded trajectories and the best sampled candidates, showing that planning errors can arise from trajectory selection, rather than trajectory generation alone. On WOD-E2E, when the ego vehicle should start moving, the model often assigns probability to different possible start times, causing greedy decoding to repeatedly favor waiting and produce a stationary plan even though moving trajectories are available. Motivated by these findings, we propose Sample-and-Verify (SaV), which keeps the VLM planner frozen and samples multiple candidate trajectories. A lightweight verifier is trained from ground-truth futures to estimate the relative quality of these candidates. It uses trajectory kinematics, candidate-set agreement and likelihood, and ego-motion features to select the final trajectory. On WOD-E2E, SaV reduces ADE and FDE by approximately 15% across three VLM planners on the validation set. Changes in RFS are smaller: two planners show slight decreases on val-456, while Alpamayo-1.5 improves from 7.74 to 7.81 RFS on the official test set as ADE@5s decreases by 11%. On NAVSIM, SaV reduces ADE@4s by 10-24% across three VLM planners, while PDMS changes are less consistent. These results show that trajectory selection can substantially improve displacement accuracy without additional planner training. The code is publicly available atthis https URL.

- **[Memory Is Not Ground Truth: Revising Navigation Authority under Unreliable VLM Evidence for Persistent Semantic Navigation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34915)**  `arXiv:2611.34915`  `cs.RO`  
  _Sumukh Shrote, Shenbagaraj Kannapiran, Eric Eaton, Camillo Jose Taylor_
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Vision-language models (VLMs) enable robots to reason about semantic and spatio-temporal context, verify task-relevant objects, and guide sensing and navigation. Persistent memory can reduce redundant search for field robots, but when should a memory continue to guide behavior? Repeated misses may suggest that a memory is stale. Our experiments show why this evidence can remain ambiguous under VLM-based perception. Using a Boston Dynamics Spot with four fixed cameras and a persistent spatio-temporal visual memory, we measure negative-verification behavior across targets, viewpoints, language queries, and repeated revisits. False-negative rates vary substantially across objects, nearby revisits often reproduce the same detector outcome, and some matched target-present and target-removed experiments yield identical miss histories. This ambiguity persists under changed observation geometry, identity-preserving query reformulation, and conditioning on a previously verified target image. These findings raise two questions: is the object still there, and is this memory still useful for navigation? We introduce a dependence-aware, reversible memory-update mechanism that avoids double-counting correlated misses and stops using unsupported memories as direct navigation goals without claiming that the object is absent. Later positive evidence can restore a memory's use for navigation. One resident onboard VLM is shared across memory verification, semantic frontier reasoning, and in-motion perception. In three matched real-robot decision contexts, an episode-local baseline selected a stale memory as its navigation objective in every case, whereas our method selected semantic frontiers in all three. A complementary trial re-verified the target during motion and promoted fresh memory. These results support revising memory actionability rather than interpreting failed detection as object absence.

  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
