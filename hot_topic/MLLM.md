- **[Which Hand Was Late? Diagnosis for Robot Failure Recovery in Bimanual Tool Use](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37533)**  `arXiv:2610.37533`  `cs.RO` `cs.AI`  
  _Baptiste Bonin, Caro Strickland, Audrey Durand_
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Failure diagnosers for robot manipulation are increasingly vision--language models (VLMs), trained and evaluated on single-arm failures produced by perturbing a successful demonstration. Bimanual tool use adds failures of other kinds: a tool that slips out of its socket while still held, two correct gestures made at different times, and attempts that fail for two reasons at once. On a simulated bimanual tool-use task whose failure causes are read from the recorded trace, we measure what each sensing channel lets a diagnoser tell. The tool's slip is read on the tool hand; which hand was late is read on both arms, while a sensor on one hand reads its own hand's delay and at most part of the other's; and whether a late attempt was also pushed too weakly is not identifiable from passive sensing when the tool hand is late. Within two attempts, a repair policy that escalates when the cause is not identifiable repairs as many failures as one told the true cause. A VLM's internal state carries the late hand almost perfectly, along a direction no closer to the one its answer reads than a random one; whether the answer reports it depends on the wording of the question.

- **[When Visual Evidence Is Insufficient: How Instructions, Post-Training, and Internal Activations Shape Refusal in Multimodal Language Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37430)**  `arXiv:2610.37430`  `cs.LG` `cs.CV`  
  _Ananth K Suresh, Aveen Dayal, Gouthaman KV, Aditya Pola, Vineeth N Balasubramanian_
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  For reliable deployment, multimodal large language models (MLLMs) should recognize when an image provides insufficient evidence to answer a question and withhold unsupported answers. Yet refusal alone is not necessarily evidence of such recognition: models may refuse because of unrelated safety constraints or without identifying the missing evidence. We study how refusal on visually unanswerable questions is shaped by three sources of control: inference-time instructions, post-training, and internal activation interventions. Across three MLLMs on the MoHoBench benchmark, we systematically manipulate the meaning and placement of refusal instructions, compare supervised and preference-based post-training, and intervene on a refusal-related activation direction derived from naturally answered and refused examples. We find that refusal is highly sensitive to both the meaning and placement of instructions, with model-specific changes at the sample level that are obscured by aggregate refusal rates. Evidence-aware and general refusal instructions have stronger effects than an intentionally mismatched safety instruction. Post-training produces larger increases in refusal, but can also induce responses that combine refusal language with attempted answers. In contrast, activation intervention produces smaller yet transferable changes across prompt conditions, without evidence of indiscriminate refusal on tested visually answerable samples. These findings show that refusal is jointly shaped by external instructions, post-training, and internal representations, highlighting the need to disentangle evidence recognition from a model's learned propensity to refuse.

  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Diffusion vision-language models (DVLMs) process their entire input at every denoising step. Since visual tokens make up 89-97% of this input on the benchmarks we study, most of the decoding computation is spent repeatedly processing the same image. Visual token pruning methods partially relieve this burden by removing part of the visual tokens, but they lose the removed information and still process the remaining image at every step. To measure how often the image is actually needed, we compare LLaDA-V's predictions with and without the image at every decoding step and find that removing the image changes a token being unmasked at only 22.8% of steps on average and at as few as 7.9%. Without training or a tuned threshold, LOWN retains 99.6% of LLaDA-V's score on ten benchmarks at 35.3% of the decoding FLOPs on average.   In video calls and livestreamed discussions, recognizing speakers' emotions helps identify emotionally salient segments and supports fine-grained video content analysis. Multimodal Emotion Recognition in Conversation (ERC) provides an approach to recognizing emotions in such content by integrating textual, visual, and acoustic information with conversational context. Current mainstream emotion recognition methods largely leverage the contextual understanding capabilities of multimodal large language models (MLLMs) to predict emotions by generating labels. However, emotion understanding and label prediction may be inconsistent, leaving the internal affective information underutilized. Moreover, when the current utterance is jointly modeled with extensive dialogue history and multimodal information, its local discriminative cues may be diluted and become less salient in the resulting contextual representation. To address these limitations, we propose CLEAR, an ERC framework that couples multimodal contextual representations with local discriminative cues. To mitigate the mismatch between emotion understanding and label prediction, we use the continuous internal representation of an emotion-supervised vision-language model (VLM) instead of its generated emotion labels, and further train a classifier based on this representation. To strengthen local discriminative cues, we use sample-adaptive residual coupling to dynamically integrate textual and acoustic evidence from the target utterance into the contextual representation. Experiments on IEMOCAP and MELD demonstrate that CLEAR achieves competitive performance, supporting the effectiveness of the proposed approach. The code is available atthis https URL.
