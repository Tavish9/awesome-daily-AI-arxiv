- **[TinyAstral: Transferring Self-Evolving Harnesses to Tiny Generalist Manipulation Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41216)**  `arXiv:2610.41216`  `cs.RO` `cs.CV`  
  _Kehong Gong, Xiaoxu Zheng, Guanli Hou, Seunghun Lee, Mengqi Guo, Runjia Tan, et al._
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Robot agents built on vision-language models depend on both the planner and its harness: the tools, feedback, memory, and recovery logic around it. Can improvements to a large teacher's harness be carried into a compact agent with lower inference compute cost? We present TinyAstral, a two-stage approach. We first freeze an open-weight teacher and improve its harness through recursive self-improvement (RSI): frontier LLM agents read the teacher's trajectories and propose harness edits, and each edit is accepted only if it passes a paired statistical test. We then freeze the final harness, collect teacher trajectories with reasoning and failure recovery, and distill a small student that replaces the teacher in the same harness. On RoboTwin, harness evolution raises the teacher's success from 33.4% to 63.6%; the final 4B student reaches 62.8%. Across four harness versions, distilled 4B students score close to their corresponding teachers, and later versions yield stronger students, showing that harness evolution gains carry into compact models. The harness gains also transfer to other frozen planners without retraining. On identical episodes, the final 4B student outperforms open 4B agents, the open 27B harness-RSI model SenseNova-RoboRSI, and our own teacher model placed in three competitors' harnesses. It stays close to its teacher on all three training benchmarks, and on three benchmarks never used for RSI or training it scores close to a 552B planner running the same harness. On the measured deployment episodes, it uses about one fifth of its teacher's accelerator time.

- **[META-Robot: Self-Cognition and Capability Scaling in Agentic Robots](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41868)**  `arXiv:2610.41868`  `cs.RO`  
  _Xuanzhi Feng, Zeyu Liu, Weiheng Gao, Xinchen Li, Jiankun Tang, Fangqi Zhu, et al._
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  LLM-based robotic agent systems can generate programs for robot control, yet their generalization to new tasks often depends on elaborate scaffolding. Expanding their skill repertoires alone offers limited guidance on how to assess new capabilities or identify what remains beyond their reach. We introduce META-Robot, a framework that couples self cognition with capability scaling by expanding the criteria through which an agent evaluates and develops its skills. At its core, action-level predicates make execution outcomes explicit and available for LLM reasoning. The agent uses these assessments to identify its own limitations, extend relevant evaluation criteria, and guide the structured expansion of its skills. Proprioceptive feedback further grounds this process in the robot's physical state, enriching the evidence available for self-cognition. Experiments in simulation and on real robots demonstrate stronger overall task performance, generalization to new tasks, continued expansion of skills and evaluation criteria, and effective use of non-visual proprioceptive signals.

  _Yihan Zeng, Yisu Li, Kaixin Huang, Marc WuDunn, Amelia Kovacs, Mikael Harseno, et al._
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Recent multimodal foundation models exhibit strong perception, reasoning, and in-context learning capabilities, but translating these capabilities into physical action remains challenging. Through real-world experiments across diverse manipulation tasks, we find that ChatGPT-6-Astra, a state-of-the-art general-purpose reasoner, can serve as a physically actionable, context-responsive manipulation policy, demonstrating effective fine manipulation and spatial reasoning. Building on these findings, we introduce LEXP, a harness for learning from physical experience through context-space adaptation. Execution traces illustrate feedback-guided corrections and cross-episode reuse of an inspection strategy. Execution analyses illustrate how task structure and experience-derived feedback guide task progression, action correction, and experience reuse. These results support context-space adaptation as a practical mechanism for turning physical interaction into reusable guidance for robotic manipulation.

  _Yijia Fan, Xilin He, Yilei Jiang, Dongming Wu, Jiaxu Wang, Xiangyu Yue_
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Across coding, web, and tool-use tasks, language-model agents are now routinely trained with the same recipe: supervised fine-tuning (SFT) to learn the interface, then multi-turn reinforcement learning (RL) to master it. Code-as-policy (CaP) casts robot manipulation in the same form, as the policy writes code against robot APIs and reads back the results, so the same recipe should apply. Yet existing CaP agents sidestep training: they either prompt closed frontier models or delegate manipulation to pre-trained vision-language-action models (VLAs) and high-level skills, while small open vision-language models (VLMs) fail outright. We therefore introduce a physical critic that reads privileged simulator state, never seen by the policy, and credits each request by how much it changes the predicted chance of success. Starting from Qwen3.5-4B (0% zero-shot), CaPtain-4B reaches 55.4% held-out LIBERO success after SFT and 73.8% after RL. On LIBERO-PRO, it reaches 78.5%, surpassing several frontier-model agents, and its RL gains persist across three perturbation suites (+17.6 to +21.2 points). Without target-domain training, it attains 83.3% on SimplerEnv WidowX, exceeds frontier models on CaP-Bench, and runs on a real robot after simulation-only adaptation.   _Xinyu Wang, Andres Fielbaum_
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>

Such rules are exactly what a large language model (LLM) can read, so drivers will be able to let one decide for them. We compare two main families of rebalancing policy once their rules are public: a reactive policy (R) that sends idle drivers to cover requests just lost, and an anticipatory policy (A) that plans moves on a demand forecast. Rebalancing policies that perform equally well under full compliance can thus show opposite results under mandatory disclosure. The study offers a framework to evaluate such policies under the new requirement and is, to our knowledge, the first to do so with LLM agents.

- **[Task Vectors Can Be Linearly Decomposed into Interpretable and Controllable Components](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41743)**  `arXiv:2610.41743`  `cs.LG` `cs.CL`  
  _Yukiya Horiba, Hakaze Cho, Takahiro Yakoh_
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Task Vectors (TVs) provide a compact way to steer large language models, but they are typically treated as atomized directions merely extracted from hidden states, leaving their internal functional structure poorly understood. Motivated by prior work that decomposes in-context learning into Task Recognition (TR) and Task Learning (TL), we ask whether a single TV can likewise be decomposed into functionally meaningful components. We formulate a TR/TL-based decomposition from candidate-label unembeddings, separating each TV into TR, TL, and residual components, and try to optimize the TR and TL coefficients using Bayesian optimization for more effective intervention. Together, these results suggest that TVs are not monolithic steering directions, but can be decomposed into functionally meaningful components that support both interpretation and control.

  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  On-policy reinforcement learning for large language models assumes that training responses are sampled from the current policy. In practice, an inference engine samples the responses and a separate trainer computes the gradients, and the \textbf{training-inference mismatch} between the two makes the training data off-policy. The mismatch has two sources, the numerics of the engine and the stale weights of asynchronous sampling, and a correction that stabilizes training under one source can fail under the other. \textit{We ask which property of a mismatch source decides the correction it needs.} Evaluating the trainer at the sampler's weights splits each sampled-token error exactly into a \textit{numerical discrepancy} of the engine and a \textit{policy lag} of the trainer, and we follow both through training.
