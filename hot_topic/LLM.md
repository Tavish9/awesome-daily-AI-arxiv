- **[SOLVE: Sparse, Ordered Keyposes Make LLM Agents Versatile and Efficient Robot Manipulator](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34750)**  `arXiv:2611.34750`  `cs.RO` `cs.CV`  
  _Xiang Li, Hongyu Zhou, Ya-Li Li, Tao Li, Rui Zhang, Junjie Lai, et al._
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  LLM agents have made remarkable progress in robot manipulation, yet they suffer from expensive inference, dense step-by-step control, and tool bottlenecks. We introduce SOLVE, a training-free, versatile and efficient agent policy: a multimodal LLM agent writes a sparse, ordered sequence of 6-DoF end-effector keyposes per call, observes the result, and writes the next, while a motion planner interpolates between them. The agent decides the number and spacing of keyposes for both fast long-range motion and fine-grained manipulation. This granularity matches the sparsity of teleoperated manipulation: most demonstrations in DROID can be compressed into a few keyposes instead of dense actions. SOLVE grounds keyposes with sparse depth queries answered by noisy sources, such as multi-view triangulation, stereo, or monocular depth, and copes with that noise itself. It also improves from execution feedback, reflecting on its own rollouts and distilling a few rules per task. SOLVE reaches state-of-the-art performance of 74.5% on RoboDojo and 84.5% on RoboLab, with only $1.7 per episode on average, 12x less than previous dense-control agent policies. On a real robot, the same agent achieves an 88% success rate (75 of 85 episodes) over 16 challenging zero-shot tasks. Our project page is atthis https URL.

- **[QK-Bench: Benchmarking Quantitative Kinematic Understanding for Dynamic Spatial Intelligence](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33991)**  `arXiv:2611.33991`  `cs.RO` `cs.CV`  
  _Lennox Wei, Jiaqi Yao, Shixin Sun, Yuanzheng Zhang, Jianhua Han, Meng Tian, et al._
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Spatial intelligence concerns both scene structure and its temporal evolution. Yet a semantically correct description can conceal a quantitative error: recognizing that objects are approaching does not determine their closing speed or whether they will meet. Such errors are critical in collision avoidance, trajectory tracking, and reactive grasping, where actions depend on motion magnitude, timing, and relative geometry. We study quantitative kinematic understanding in multimodal large language models (MLLMs): resolving motion magnitude, rate, reference-frame dependence, and future spatial evolution from visual evidence. We introduce the Quantitative Kinematics Benchmark (QK-Bench), comprising 2,341 multiple-choice QA pairs from 379 videos and 17 tasks spanning Object-Centric Kinematics, Camera Ego-Motion Kinematics, and Multi-Object Kinematic Relations. Hidden geometric and temporal signals support trajectory construction and filtering, while fixed kinematic rules map physical quantities to verifiable answers, including categorical labels. Trajectory and question validation ensure target reliability and sufficient visual support in the sampled frames. All models receive a unified monocular RGB input. Evaluations of proprietary, open-source, and spatial MLLMs reveal substantial limitations: the strongest model achieves only 41.2\% overall accuracy, and spatial MLLMs show no consistent advantage. These findings indicate that current advances in spatial intelligence do not reliably transfer to quantitative kinematic understanding.

- **[SEEK: Self-Evolution through Execution Knowledge for Agentic Robot Manipulation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33915)**  `arXiv:2611.33915`  `cs.RO` `cs.CV`  
  _Zeyang Guan, Qihao Zhao, Rui Gong, Zhang Haitian, Yalun Dai, Yangyu Huang, et al._
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  General-purpose multimodal foundation models enable robots to adapt during deployment from observations and execution feedback, without task-specific demonstrations or training. However, the robot retains what it learns only in the model's working context. Once the task ends, it loses this execution knowledge, such as how to carry a bowl between two grippers. On the next attempt at the task, the robot may take just as long and repeat the same mistakes. To avoid this repetition, robots must retain execution knowledge across attempts. Yet replaying past actions is insufficient, because their effects depend on the current conditions. Procedures that determine actions from the current scene address this, but when they meet conditions that earlier attempts did not cover, revising them requires the measured effects of earlier actions. We therefore propose SEEK, an agentic framework that retains both, enabling robots to improve across attempts while keeping the underlying model fixed. To adapt retained knowledge to the current scene, SEEK adopts a Self-Evolving Programmatic Policy (SEPP) that encodes manipulation as executable procedures and determines each action from current observations. As the robot executes these procedures, SEEK records the measured effects of its actions, including those from failed attempts, in a Persistent Action-Effect Bank (PAEB). Where a retained procedure applies, it acts without a model request; where it meets a condition it does not cover, this evidence guides its revision. On RoboDojo, knowledge retained over ten attempts raises SEEK's average task score from 27.0 to 54.4 and its success rate from 19.7% to 48.0% across all 42 tasks. On 34 of these tasks with GPT-6.2 Astra, SEEK reaches 80.1 after five attempts, against 49.6 for the same model acting directly, and uses 77.6% fewer model requests.this https URL

- **[RoboThink: Let the Agent Think, Let the Policy Act](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.30111)**  `arXiv:2611.30111`  `cs.RO` `cs.AI`  
  _Yunbei Zhang, Zijian Jin, Janet Wang, Xingchen Zhao, Zhenyu Zhang, Weirui Ye, et al._
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Robot policies act in real time but fail without knowing why, whereas a reasoning agent that watches the robot can often tell what went wrong. Existing systems either hand the agent control, which takes minutes per episode, or let it send instructions. Yet a policy follows a new instruction only from a fresh start, since a half-finished task leaves it in states outside its training data. Nor can an instruction fix how the robot moves. If a drift keeps pulling the hand to the left, "push right, slightly" is not a task the policy has learned. We propose RoboThink, in which the agent thinks and the policy learns to act on the agent's guidance. An instruction channel tells the policy what to do. When the user says "the milk, not the butter" after the robot has started, the agent rewrites the instruction and the robot undoes its own motion, with no training. On LIBERO, RoboThink with Claude Opus 5.5 raises success from 0.09 to 0.87 under an unknown constant drift and from 0.42 to 0.84 under a random one. It also completes misheard and changed requests in 0.91 to 1.00 of the episodes. Acting alone, GPT-6 Astra needs 2.1 min per success and over a million tokens per episode, whereas RoboThink needs 55 s and 8k tokens. The same recipe works across policies, for both vision-language-action models and world action models, and across robots, from one arm to two arms and a mobile manipulator. On all 50 RoboTwin 2.0 tasks with two arms, success rises from 0.12 to 0.74 under the constant drift and from 0.31 to 0.56 under the random one. Finally, the agent is a plug-in. A policy taught by one agent works with other agents, including open-weight ones, and grows stronger with a stronger agent, without retraining.
