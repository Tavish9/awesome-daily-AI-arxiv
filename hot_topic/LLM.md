- **[Training Reasoning Models in Simulation to Natively Use Real Robots](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18562)**  `arXiv:2611.18562`  `cs.RO` `cs.LG`  
  _Jiaming Tang, Zhuoyang Zhang, Junxian Guo, Fangzhou Zhao, Yilong Zhao, Zekai Li, et al._
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Robot-use agents (RUAs) have emerged as a promising approach to general-purpose robot control. However, strong performance has so far been achieved only by frontier proprietary models such as GPT-6 Astra and Claude Fable 5.2, whereas open-weight models remain substantially less capable. We show that an open-weight 27B reasoning model can be trained to use real robots natively through reinforcement learning, entirely in simulation and without demonstrations. Like a computer-use agent (CUA), the model observes the environment, reasons in a chain of thought, and generates the next end-effector pose, without any harness. We build a Post-training Engine for Agentic Robotics (PEAR) and use it to train the model on simulated robot tasks at scale with robot-use reinforcement learning (RURL), mirroring how frontier models acquire computer use. We find that the skills acquired in simulation generalize well beyond the training tasks: they transfer to an unseen robot, to the real world, and to novel instructions. After training on LIBERO and RoboDojo in simulation, the model succeeds in 60% of our trials on a real dual-arm robot, whereas the base model succeeds in none. On LIBERO, training raises the success rate from 1.6% to 74.4%, approaching GPT-6 Astra and GPT-6.1 Sol at maximum reasoning effort. Compared with $\pi_{0.5}$, a vision-language-action model fine-tuned on demonstrations, our model is substantially more robust to changes in the task itself. On LIBERO-Pro, $\pi_{0.5}$ succeeds in only 0.75% of episodes when assigned a new task in a familiar scene whereas our model succeeds in 57.4%. Finally, the model retains its general capabilities, with no measurable degradation on nine reasoning, coding and vision benchmarks. These results highlight robot-use reinforcement learning as a promising component of foundation-model post-training.

- **[Racing the Clock: Teaching VLNs Budget-Conditioned Object Search](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17442)**  `arXiv:2611.17442`  `cs.RO` `cs.AI` `cs.CV` `cs.LG`  
  _Maitree Meher, Siming He, S. Shankar Sastry, Jitendra Malik, Claire Tomlin, Jonas Frey_
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  A rescue robot entering a building with thirty seconds on the clock should sprint toward the single most promising room; given ten minutes, it should instead explore a broader tour. Yet vision-language-navigation (VLN) models are typically evaluated under a fixed episode limit that the policy never observes, even though time-critical missions require behavior to adapt to the available budget. We study budget-conditioned object search: given a target category and an explicit action budget, an agent must find as many distinct target instances as possible before the budget expires. We investigate how to supervise VLNs for this task by comparing a privileged TSP expert, which plans near-optimal routes using the full map and true target locations, with a room-level Bellman expert that maximizes expected return under partial observability using high-level cross-scene semantic priors. Despite the TSP expert achieving substantially higher expert performance, its demonstrations are less effective for learning exploration: on held-out HM3D scenes, the Bellman-supervised VLN consistently outperforms the TSP-supervised VLN, with the advantage becoming more pronounced at longer budgets. At 2000 steps (500m), the Bellman-supervised VLN finds 71.4% of reachable target instances, compared with 65.5% for the TSP-supervised VLN and 44.8% for the pretrained VLN. These results highlight a distinction between optimal routing and optimal exploration: effective exploration supervision should optimize decisions under partial observability rather than imitate routes that are optimal only once the environment is fully known.

Within weeks, frontier models with agentic harnesses matched or exceeded task-specific policies on many embodied benchmarks. Instead of training a dedicated policy for each family of tasks, an agentic robotic system applies a foundation model to open-world embodied tasks. - **[ESI: An Embodied Self-Improvement Flywheel for LLM-VLA Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18176)**  `arXiv:2611.18176`  `cs.RO`  
  _Yundi Cai, Zihan Wang, Chenxi Han, Yi Cheng, Houde Liu, Zilei Wang_
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Independently pretrained language models and vision-language-action (VLA) policies provide complementary building blocks for embodied agents, but connecting them does not automatically produce coordinated behavior. The VLA may generate actions that diverge from the LLM's intended subtask and, upon regaining control after a correction, undo the promising state restored by the LLM. We introduce ESI (Embodied Self-Improvement), an automated flywheel that uses environmental interaction to align reasoning and action. ESI treats the modular agent as the unit of post-training, repeatedly refining the prompts that guide the LLM, the shared skill interface that expresses its intent, and the VLA parameters that realize that intent, then redeploying these components together. On ten selected RoboDojo tasks, ESI achieves a mean Score of 62.03 and a mean success rate of 49.41% using an LLM-VLA agent without generating task-specific control code. On both metrics, it outperforms the published reference results of all hybrid embodied agents, including the Agent+WAM system GO-3 Nano Preview (47.27 Score and 36.60% success), as well as all compared code-based methods.

- **[Evaluating Language Models as Spatial Priors for Object-Goal Navigation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18123)**  `arXiv:2611.18123`  `cs.RO`  
  _Jun Miura_
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Semantic exploration methods for object-goal navigation use large language models (LLMs) to score how strongly a target object is associated with a room type or with a nearby object. This paper considers obtaining from the LLM a spatial prior instead: a distribution over the position of the target in a room. We evaluate whether two open-weight LLMs, Qwen3.5-27B and Gemma-4-31B-it, can provide such a prior. Using real room layouts from CubiCasa5K and ScanNet, we measure the likelihood the models assign to real and perturbed layouts, and the layouts they generate. The object frequencies and room sizes of the generated rooms follow the room type, but the proximity of the objects to the walls does not follow the differences between room types found in real rooms. The likelihood distinguishes real layouts from perturbed ones partly through their geometry and partly through the agreement between the coordinates and a field that states the wall each object touches; for the movable furniture of ScanNet, it is higher for layouts with all objects piled near the room center than for the real layouts in most rooms. When an object is given in the prompt, the models place a related object relative to it. When the wall of each object is given, they place most objects against that wall, but do not keep all objects inside a given room rectangle. As a prior for object search, the generated placements reduce the searched area relative to a uniform prior, but by less than a third of the reduction obtained with a prior built from real layouts, and by less than two thirds when both priors are spatially smoothed. These results support the use of LLMs for object-room associations and for writing coordinates under constraints determined by another component, followed by a geometric check, but not as a source of placement priors where real layouts are available.

  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Anomaly detection in tabular data poses significant challenges due to heterogeneous feature types-mixing numerical, categorical, and textual attributes, which complicate learning meaningful representations of normality. - **[Deep Latents, Long Memory: Latent persistence explains temporal correlations in transformer activations](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18089)**  `arXiv:2611.18089`  `cs.LG` `cs.CL`  
  _Francesco Cagnetta, Santiago Acevedo_
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  The hidden layers of Large Language Models (LLMs) are believed to encode representations of the data whose abstraction increases with depth. However, in the absence of an operational notion of abstraction, this statement is hard to test quantitatively. Here, we propose an alternative view, building on the principle that the correlations of a sequence reflect the structure of the process that generated it. We define the autocorrelation function of a layer as the average dot product between its activations at two context positions separated by a lag. Latent variables encoded persistently along the context create slowly decaying tails in the autocorrelation, and we find these tails to grow across layers in both synthetic and natural data. In synthetic data with controlled hierarchical latent structure, this growth stems from the encoding of abstract latent variables, which govern long stretches of the input. Indeed, the persistence of latent codes predicts the qualitative structure of autocorrelations and accounts for most of the empirical measures. In real text, we take sparse-autoencoder features as proxies for latent variables, and find that their persistence correlates strongly with the magnitude of autocorrelations across datasets and LLMs. In particular, the persistence time increases with depth, as do autocorrelations, implying that deeper latent features persist for longer context spans. We complement this analysis by explicitly ablating the persistent sparse-autoencoder features and observing a consequent decrease in the autocorrelations.
