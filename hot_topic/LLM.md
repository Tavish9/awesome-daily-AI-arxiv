- **[Can LLMs Debug Robots by Editing Their Weights?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09376)**  `arXiv:2611.09376`  `cs.RO` `cs.AI`  
  _Jusheng Zhang, Keze Wang, Liang Lin_
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  A language model can repair a robot policy's lexical instruction grounding by editing just 65 token-embedding rows. We bring weight-space programming to robot foundation policies: GPT-6 Astra reads weights and uses a CPU workspace and budgeted simulator feedback, without gradient updates or new demonstrations. Three independent sessions on $\pi_{0.5}$ all improve success on 765 held-out paraphrases; the best raises success from 86.3\% to 92.0\% (48 repairs, four regressions; exact paired $p=1.31\times10^{-10}$). Among the 335 instructions containing edited tokens, success rises from 74.3\% to 87.5\%; the other 430 episodes have zero outcome changes. Matched random-direction edits do not help. Full replacement using the editor's own lexicon explains much of the gain but produces more regressions; its direct difference from the editor is not significant at 0.05. Across 13 further robot sessions, the editor declines 12 candidates; the sole retained edit does not show a significant held-out gain. Separately, benchmark metadata lowers FastWAM success from 70.0\% to 48.6\%, while the evaluated VLA's aggregate rate is unchanged. The evidence supports precise lexical repair, not general acquisition of missing robot capabilities.

- **[EvoPlan: Evolutionary Neuro-Symbolic Robot Planning with Spatio-Temporal Guarantees](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.06724)**  `arXiv:2607.06724`  `cs.RO`  
  _Bhavya Sai Nukapotula, Samin Moosavi, Haoze Wang, Luke Duncan, Diya Shakkottai, Varun Murali, et al._
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  LLM-based robot planners are fluent but cannot guarantee that their plans are executable or safe. Classical PDDL planners can guarantee symbolic validity, but only once the problem is fully specified, and they make poor use of an LLM's ability to read context and repair plans. We present EvoPlan, a neuro-symbolic framework whose guarantees are symbolic validity and satisfaction of a constraint learned from data. The planner works with locally-hosted open-weight models, so it does not require a cloud service. EvoPlan has three parts. First, an offline procedure that mines a single global Signal Temporal Logic (STL) mobility constraint from demonstrations. Perturbed demonstrations supply the missing negatives without encoding any rule, and evolutionary search fits the constraint, which captures driving rules or social-navigation preferences within a modelled taxonomy of norms. We use the mined constraint to shield driving and navigation policies on Bench2Drive and HA-VLN-CE. Second, an evolutionary PDDL planner: an LLM proposes and repairs plans, programmatic validators decide which ones survive, and the validated portion of the plan grows over iterations. On open-world ALFWorld and NL-PDDL Blocksworld, it approaches classical planning when the goal vocabulary agrees with the action model, and it stays robust when the two disagree and classical planning fails. Third, a constrained execution loop: Nav2 uses the costmap to ground the plan into waypoints, which are checked against the mined constraint. Violations and execution failures update the PDDL problem for re-planning. We demonstrate the full pipeline in Gazebo and on a Clearpath Jackal robot. - **[Phantom Consensus: Measuring Social Reasoning in Multi-Agent LLM Decision-Making](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08971)**  `arXiv:2611.08971`  `cs.MA` `cs.AI` `cs.CL`  
  _Disha Sheshanarayana, Rajat Subhra Pal, Manjira Sinha, Tirthankar Dasgupta_
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Multi-agent LLM systems increasingly aggregate judgments across model instances, but reasoning about other agents can itself introduce failure. We study a setting in which LLMs shift their public decisions after observing ambiguous social signals while reporting inaccurate estimates of what other agents privately judged, even when the group's recorded private judgments do not support the apparent consensus. We call this failure phantom consensus and study it as a pluralistic-ignorance-style pattern in controlled LLM collectives. To measure this phenomenon, we introduce PHANTOM-Bench, a benchmark that separates isolated private judgments, public decisions after ambiguous social signals, elicited estimates of the group's private judgment, transparency-based recovery, and controls for neutral public text and explicit vote-following. Across nine closed- and open-weight models, ambiguous public signals consistently degrade decision quality and can turn correct private group judgments into incorrect public collective outcomes. Public accuracy drops by as much as 47.8 percentage points, and collective rationality failures reach 47.3% in the most susceptible setting. We observe substantial errors in reported group-private beliefs alongside public-stage failures, while control conditions reveal model-dependent differences in susceptibility to ambiguous and explicit peer signals. Revealing the agents' recorded private decisions repairs many failures without revealing the gold label, although recovery varies across models. Our results show that evaluating multi-agent LLM systems requires measuring not only final agreement, but whether that agreement reflects independently measured private judgments or an apparent consensus.

- **[When Disclosure Is Compulsory: Evaluating Ride-Hailing Rebalancing Policies with Self-Interested LLM Driver Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41132)**  `arXiv:2610.41132`  `cs.MA` `cs.AI`  
  _Xinyu Wang, Andres Fielbaum_
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  The EU Platform Work Directive, to be transposed by December 2026, requires ride-hailing platforms to state in plain language the main parameters of their automated decisions and how a worker's behaviour affects them. Such rules are exactly what a large language model (LLM) can read, so drivers will be able to let one decide for them. We compare two main families of rebalancing policy once their rules are public: a reactive policy (R) that sends idle drivers to cover requests just lost, and an anticipatory policy (A) that plans moves on a demand forecast. Each driver is an LLM agent maximizing its own net income, given no rules (N) or the rules at the directive's level of detail (L). We test two LLMs, Claude Haiku 5.5 and Jev, which understand the rules equally well, in a three-zone model and in Manhattan. With the rules disclosed, drivers follow the platform more closely: in Manhattan, the share of relocation offers they decline falls from about four in five to one in four under both policies. Yet the effects differ by policy. For the platform, disclosure costs R 3.7% of its profit in Manhattan, as R now pays for moves drivers used to make for free, while A's profit is unchanged; the two policies, which tie on profit under full compliance, end 6.1% apart. For drivers, disclosure raises income in the small model under both policies and LLMs; for users, it raises the service rate there by 10 to 18 percentage points. In Manhattan, self-interested drivers also make A a win-win-win against full compliance, raising driver income, platform profit and service rate by 10 to 12% each. Rebalancing policies that perform equally well under full compliance can thus show opposite results under mandatory disclosure. The study offers a framework to evaluate such policies under the new requirement and is, to our knowledge, the first to do so with LLM agents.

  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
