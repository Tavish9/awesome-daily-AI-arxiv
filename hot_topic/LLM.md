- **[Multi-Tool Selection for Robotic Systems: Comparing Semantic Retrieval and LLM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31013)**  `arXiv:2610.31013`  `cs.RO` `cs.AI`  
  _Miguel Sousa, Luis Ramos Pinto, João P. Matos-Carvalho_
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Natural-language interfaces allow users to command robotic systems without interacting directly with mission planners or robotics middleware. Their reliable use depends on robot tool routing, defined here as the selection of the smallest complete subset of predefined executable capabilities required to satisfy a request. This task is particularly difficult for compound instructions, where incomplete selection may omit necessary sensing or monitoring capabilities, while excessive selection may expose irrelevant tools. We compare four routing strategies: three progressively refined ChromaDB-based methods - fixed-threshold retrieval, relative-distance retrieval, and clause-aware aggregated retrieval - and a cloud-hosted 120-billion-parameter language-model baseline. The methods are evaluated on 164 manually annotated prompts organised into three complexity levels, from direct single-tool requests to compound requests requiring at least three tools. Using exact-match accuracy, completeness, precision, a completeness-weighted composite score, and routing latency, CDB-v3 achieves the best macro-average score among the retrieval-only methods, with 0.7822, compared with 0.8869 for the large-model baseline. Although the LLM provides higher routing quality, CDB-v3 exhibits substantially lower latency and can be deployed locally without requiring cloud connectivity. These results identify a quality-latency trade-off between direct large-model selection and clause-aware semantic retrieval for privacy-sensitive, connectivity-constrained, and resource-constrained robotic systems.

  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Vehicle-to-everything (V2X) communication enables connected vehicles to make safety-critical decisions, but delayed, missing, or incorrect information can compromise driving safety. Systematically testing such failures is challenging because traffic interactions, communication conditions, and application responses must be jointly configured to produce meaningful scenarios. This paper presents LAST-V2X, a language-driven, adaptive framework for automated scenario synthesis and safety testing of V2X applications. LAST-V2X leverages large language models (LLMs) to translate natural-language test descriptions into executable scenarios grounded in roadway geometry, V2X communication semantics, and vehicle behavior.   <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>

While Large Language Models (LLMs) introduce a new paradigm for TAMP by leveraging extensive common sense, their inherent reasoning limitations hinder direct application, and fine-tuning strategies to enhance these capabilities are currently bottlenecked by the scarcity of high-quality datasets.   <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>

- **[Poisoning-Based Backdoors Are Not Cryptographic](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.28045)**  `arXiv:2610.28045`  `cs.LG` `cs.CL`  
  _Masahiro Kaneko_
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Defending against a backdoor requires recovering the verifier that decides whether an input is a trigger, since blocking individual triggers fails against an attacker who can generate new ones. If a verifier could be installed with the asymmetry of cryptographic authentication, where only the attacker can produce triggers and the defender cannot reconstruct the verifier, defense that learns the verifier from observations would be impossible in principle. We show that no such asymmetry can arise when the verifier enters training only through the labels of poisoned data, which covers the dirty-label backdoors that dominate the literature on language models. A training procedure that installs a verifier from poisoned data is itself a learner for that verifier. Hence any verifier that is hard to learn cannot be installed through poisoning, and any verifier that poisoning can install is reconstructible by a white-box defender holding as many labeled trigger observations as the attacker used. Hiding a secret key in the input or the weights does not change this. Experiments on instruction-tuned large language models confirm the dichotomy, with no configuration yielding a backdoor that is both effective for the attacker and irrecoverable for the defender.
  </details>

- **[Rubric Dropout: A Simple Way to Mitigate Reward Hacking in Rubric-Based RL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.11669)**  `arXiv:2608.11669`  `cs.LG` `cs.AI` `cs.CL`

  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Rubric-based reinforcement learning, in which an LLM judge scores responses against prompt-specific criteria, has become a standard way to post-train language models on tasks without verifiable answers. The rubric, however, is only a proxy for response quality, and a policy trained against it long enough learns to exploit the difference. We train models with GRPO on medical and science rubrics and grade their responses on out-of-distribution (OOD) benchmarks throughout training with two judges: the training judge and a stronger OOD judge. The two scores first rise together and then diverge. The policy is learning to satisfy the training judge without writing better responses, which is a form of reward hacking. We also introduce Block Dropout, which drops semantically similar criteria together so that a near duplicate of a dropped criterion cannot keep rewarding the same behavior. Across both benchmarks and models from 1.7B to 8B, dropout improves the gold judge's score on OOD benchmarks by up to 3 percent on HealthBench-Hard and 7 percent on ResearchQA, and it reduces both the gap between the two judges and over-crediting. It keeps in-domain reward close to the baseline, and dropout rates of 30-50 percent work best. Block Dropout improves the gold judge's OOD score further at the same dropout rate. Changing which criteria are rewarded at each step is a simple, inexpensive way to make rubric rewards harder to exploit.
