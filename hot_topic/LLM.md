  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  A single policy that controls diverse robots must not only transfer task knowledge across embodiments, but also adapt its behavior to each robot's capabilities. Existing approaches facilitate transfer through shared action representations, but these representations do not necessarily capture how differences in robot morphology should shape manipulation strategies. We introduce OmniAct, a cross-embodiment manipulation policy that conditions directly on each robot's kinematics in a specific scene to produce embodiment-specific behavior. This kinematic graph represents the robot's joint positions, motion axes, and connectivity to its visual observation, allowing the policy to account for how its embodiment related to objects and surfaces in the scene. - **[RoboInF: A Benchmark for Instruction Fidelity in Robotic Manipulation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.30555)**  `arXiv:2611.30555`  `cs.RO` `cs.AI`  
  _Jiawei Guo, Yu Huang, Dijiang Dong, Yue Liu, Peiran Ren, Wei Shen_
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Precise language control requires embodied models to follow instructions governing both task outcomes and execution. Although recent methods train policies to follow increasingly detailed commands, existing benchmarks often assess instruction following alongside perception, planning, and generalization to unfamiliar scenes. Higher task success therefore offers limited evidence of closer adherence to instructions. We introduce RoboInF, a benchmark measuring how reliably manipulation policies adapt their behavior to changes in an instruction. For each of eight tasks, six to twelve expert-validated instruction branches vary the requested goal, procedure, or manipulation detail while holding the initial scene and robot state fixed. Task-specific checkers verify the terminal goal and use recorded trajectories, contacts, and event sequences to assess compliance throughout execution. The accompanying RoboInF-4K dataset pairs 4,242 expert demonstrations with simulator-derived annotations of motion and contact. We evaluate leading manipulation policies ($\pi_{0.5}$ and OpenWAM) and inference-only agents (GPT-6 Astra and Qwen3.8-Flash). All four models exhibit a gap between task completion and faithful execution, indicating substantial room for fine-grained instruction following.

- **[Any2Act: Scaling In-Context Robot Learning from Heterogeneous Demonstrations](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.32353)**  `arXiv:2611.32353`  `cs.RO`  
  _Ziang Ren, Zifan Wang, Zhuoyang Li, Benhao Qin, Haoyang Li, Qingtao Liu, et al._
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Robot in-context learning (ICL) enables a policy to perform unseen tasks by following contextual demonstrations, without task-specific fine-tuning. This is increasingly valuable as large language models reasoning at low frequency can specify tasks through demonstrations and delegate high-frequency execution to such a policy. However, methods conditioned on human videos are bounded by scarce human-robot pairs, while those conditioned on robot demonstrations accept only one or two source types at limited scale. We present Any2Act, a foundation model for robot ICL. Our key insight is that a demonstration should be expressed in the camera layout and action space of the robot that performs it (the executor), not of whoever provided it (the demonstrator). This unified embodiment-grounded representation decouples learning to follow from admitting a new source. Any2Act learns to follow once, from robot-to-robot pairs that already take this form in 1.2M real trajectories spanning 10K tasks. Human videos, simulation, UMI, and other robots are then translated into this representation, and source-routed adapters trained on only a few hundred pairs each absorb the residual gap while the pretrained model stays frozen. For long horizons, a training-free tracker feeds only the demonstration window around the executor's progress, bounding the context and rolling back to redo failed steps. From a single demonstration, Any2Act achieves 57.5% success on unseen tasks without fine-tuning, exceeding VLAs fine-tuned on 50 demonstrations per task; other demonstrators retain 74-91% of the success achieved with the executor's own demonstrations.

  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Large language models increasingly work as teams of agents, and a team is expected to solve problems beyond the reach of any single member. Most evaluations credit a team for outperforming a single agent, without accounting for the additional computation: a team may consume many times the tokens of one agent, and a plain vote over independent answers already explains most of the reported gain. Two questions therefore remain open: When does interaction itself make a group smarter, and can a group improve its next generation by learning from its own work? We answer both with the equal-budget ladder, which grades a group against independent agents at three levels: outperforming a single agent, a compute-matched majority vote, and compute-matched pass@n. Third, groups recursively improve themselves: Two label-free generations progressively climb the ladder, the second 9B generation surpassing both pass@$n$ and a control trained on base-model turns; on PlanBench, HiddenBench and Hanabi, each generation outperforms its predecessor on unseen problems for four to six generations.   Open-weight LLMs are competent software engineers, but they sometimes fail at tasks they are otherwise able to do. Often it is not a failure of reasoning. It is a few small missteps over a long trajectory (repeating a step that has already failed, or declaring the task done while a requirement is still unmet) that make the difference. Correcting this calls for a surgical weight update that steers the model toward the right actions without interfering with its reasoning. Such updates, typically learned with LoRA adapters, are often too small to survive merging into a released checkpoint. In our BF16 release, the median weight change is only 1.5% of a single rounding step, and merging loses roughly two thirds of the update's norm. When the same adapter is merged into the mixed FP8/NVFP4 release of the same model, 93% of the update disappears. Instead of increasing the size of the update, we directly train on the surviving part. We introduce merge-aware fine-tuning. Standard LoRA rounds the merged weights to the storage format only at export, so the loss never sees the rounded weights. We instead round the sum of base weights and adapter update to the release format at every training step, keeping the vendor's quantization scales, and compute the loss on these weights.
