- **[RoboHarness: A Simple Harness Outperforms VLA and World Action Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.14018)**  `arXiv:2610.14018`  `cs.RO` `cs.AI`

- **[HexaShell: A Thin Harness for Exploring the Limits of VLM Agents as Robot Coding Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.23479)**  `arXiv:2610.23479`  `cs.RO`  
  _Hongcheng Gao, Alex Zhou, Jay Zhu, Zelin Zheng, Shijia Ge, Xiping Li, et al._
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Agents built on vision language models (VLMs) now compete with learned policies on robot manipulation leaderboards. We ask how far a coding agent can go when it controls a robot instead of a computer, and where it stops. Our system, HexaShell, keeps the harness thin: it only turns the agent's commands into robot motion. The agent drives a robot with two arms through one command line. It sees three RGB cameras and knows their calibration. After each failed attempt, the agent reads the log, finds the cause, and writes or fixes small programs called tools. In this way it keeps what it learns as code, as a coding agent does when it fixes a failing test. The tools may use only what the robot observes. They use triangulation to find where objects are, relative to each other and to the gripper. If two cameras see the same point, the point lies where the two camera rays meet. If a point lies on a known plane, such as the table or the top of a block, it lies where its ray meets that plane. When the head camera is not enough, the robot moves a wrist camera to get a second view. We build the tools on only ten layouts of one seed per task, then freeze them and test them on ten new layouts of the same seed. We use Codex with GPT-6-Astra at medium reasoning effort. On all 42 RoboDojo tasks, HexaShell reaches a success rate of 39.7% and a progress score of 48.0, averaged in the same way as the leaderboard. This is a new state of the art on RoboDojo. The limit is contact, not reasoning: pushing reaches 92.5% and memory tasks 65.0%, but tight insertion only 8.3%. When the agent fails, it usually sees the failure but cannot make the motion work. We open source the tools evolved so far atthis https URL.

  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Vision-and-Language Navigation (VLN) requires embodied agents to ground language in visual observations and make long-horizon decisions. Existing methods often address these demands through task-specific architectures. As multimodal large language models (MLLMs) become increasingly capable, an important question remains: Have we truly unlocked the potential of native MLLMs for VLN before introducing task-specific architecture? We present SimpleVLN, an architecture-preserving framework developed through studies of navigation formulation, data construction, and optimization.   <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Quadruped parkour policies handle canonical obstacles well, but their training curricula often miss the irregular, compound geometry of industrial and disaster-response sites, where agile locomotion matters most.   _Xiao Huang, Mingda Zhang, Junming Zhang, Qiang Huang, Hanwen Zhang, Yue Dai, et al._
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Recursive self-improvement (RSI) lets a system improve from its own outcomes; in LLM-based multi-agent systems, Agents refine one another within a task, and outcomes improve how they collaborate across tasks. However, existing multi-agent collaboration leaves this loop open: collaboration is pre-defined at the operator level, topology-only learning keeps verbatim exchange that propagates errors, and reward maximization on a system's own outcomes concentrates on a few teams.   _Hyeong Kyu Choi, Jiatong Li, Wendi Li, Xin Eric Wang, Sharon Li_
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Exploration is essential for reliable autonomy in multi-agent systems, yet it remains unclear whether large language model (LLM) agents can explore effectively when interacting with one another. We show that modern LLM agents often fail to do so, exhibiting myopic and polarized interaction patterns that lead to suboptimal coordination and increased regret. We formalize this challenge as the Multi-Agent Exploration problem, modeling it as a partially observable stochastic game (POSG), in which agents must probe peers to infer their capabilities and identify effective interaction strategies. - **[Where Do Multi-Agent Systems Fail? Evidence-Grounded Diagnosis of Collective Mechanisms](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.38761)**  `arXiv:2609.38761`  `cs.MA`  
  _Zhengye Han_
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  When a multi-agent system answers correctly, it is tempting to conclude that its agents shared, checked, and used information as intended. Yet a system can break one of its collective mechanisms, the rules that govern how agents route, admit, store, and act on shared information, and still return the right answer, while a wrong answer rarely reveals which mechanism failed. We ask what evidence from an execution is sufficient to conclude that a particular mechanism was violated. The contracts also applied, in narrow form, to mechanisms in independently developed systems, but a diagnostic behavior that was nearly perfect on our benchmark degraded on an independently developed workflow. A correct outcome is therefore no substitute for records of how collective mechanisms operated, and agreement on one benchmark does not show that a diagnoser transfers to another system.

- **[Sample, Mix, or Verify? Allocating Test-Time Compute under Imperfect Verifiers](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.23255)**  `arXiv:2610.23255`  `cs.LG` `cs.CL`  
  _Srinivasan Iyengar_
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  A common way to make language models and agents more accurate is to spend more compute at inference time: generate many candidate solutions and submit the one a verifier prefers. Success then requires that some candidate is correct and that the verifier picks it. The first grows predictably with the number of samples; the second does not, because real verifiers (unit tests, majority votes, LLM judges, reward models) accept wrong answers, reject right ones, and make correlated mistakes. With a fixed budget, a practitioner must decide how many samples to draw, which models to draw them from, and how much to spend on verification, and today these choices are made by rules of thumb. We introduce SPLIT, which makes them from a small labelled pilot. It rests on an exact formula for the probability of submitting a correct answer under accept/reject and top-score selection that accounts for varying task difficulty, blind spots shared across models, and correlated repeated verifier calls. Regret depends only on how well SPLIT ranks configurations, and it stays near-optimal under compute, price and latency costs and online. The formula also explains when asking more LLM judges lowers precision. As a forecaster far beyond the pilot, SPLIT beats power-law fitting when the verifier caps success, and we report where it loses.
