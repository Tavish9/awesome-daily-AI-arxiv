- **[Beyond Prediction Gain: LASR as a Metric for Action-Grounded Latents in World Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17775)**  `arXiv:2611.17775`  `cs.RO` `cs.LG`  
  _Yu Zhou, Changsong Lei, Yixiang Wei, Kairui Wen, Huankun Sheng, Ming Li, et al._
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  World models that support planning, control, and interpretable reasoning require latent representations that genuinely encode action. Yet the large-scale video data from which such models are learned overwhelmingly lack action annotations. Latent-action models infer action-like variables from observation sequences, enabling action-aware learning from unlabeled videos or trajectories. A fundamental concern, however, remains: a latent that improves future prediction may simply encode the current state, episode progress, task identity, or other predictive shortcuts. Existing evaluation practices---reconstruction loss, rollout fidelity, action recovery, or downstream performance---cannot isolate this distinction. We introduce the Latent Action Support Ratio (LASR), a dimensionless metric that measures, under offline evaluation on held-out data, whether a learned latent provides predictive support for action-related targets beyond the current state and non-action alternatives. LASR provides two complementary summaries: GeoLASR captures typical support, while WorstLASR identifies the weakest case. We instantiate the LASR benchmark with recorded actions and corresponding physical state changes as targets, episode-held-out splits, one predictor type for all comparisons, and positive and negative controls. Experiments on recorded robot manipulation data and a matched multi-task panel demonstrate that LASR detects known action information, rejects representative non-action shortcuts, and enables graded comparisons among imperfect latent representations. We further use LASR to train and audit a lightweight residual adapter, obtaining descriptively better point estimates whose uncertainty is assessed with paired comparisons. LASR thus provides a principled offline protocol for measuring action-related predictive support in world-model latents.

- **[Imitation Policies Learn a Proprioceptive Anchor: A Predictive Law for Perception Noise and Its Closed Loop Cost](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17658)**  `arXiv:2611.17658`  `cs.RO` `cs.AI` `cs.LG`  
  _Christopher Malili_
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Imitation policies for robot manipulation are often trained on noisy object positions, and they learn to discount the noisy reading. We show how much they discount it, and why. Because demonstrators steer toward the object, the robot's own state carries information about where the object is. A trained policy combines this proprioceptive anchor with the reading and weights the reading by $\alpha = V/(V + s^2)$, where $s^2$ is the reading noise and $V$ the anchor's error variance. $V$ belongs to the learner and is far smaller than a linear model of the demonstrations implies, which is why classical errors-in-variables (EIV) regression overestimates $\alpha$. Measured once, $V$ predicted ten held-out sensitivities across two robomimic tasks, two learners and four new noise levels, with mean absolute error 0.025 against 0.37 for classical EIV, and a parameter-free bias test rules out the simpler explanation that the policies just overestimate the noise. The discount does not carry over to deployment, where the policy produces its own state: closed-loop sensitivity to a persistent reading offset exceeded $\alpha$ in all 15 policies that passed the registered gates, although the stricter registered prediction, that all would follow the offset fully, held for 8 of them. Post hoc, lower $\alpha$ went with lower success, steeply for diffusion, and a registered prospective test confirmed this for diffusion on Can at two new noise levels: the measured $\alpha$ predicted success before any rollout, within 0.03 (95% intervals $\pm 0.08$), although training noise predicts it equally well. Every test was registered before its data, including the classical benchmark that failed, and all other analyses are labelled post hoc; all results are in simulation with synthetic Gaussian noise.
  </details>

  _Spencer Teetaert, Sven Lilge, Jessica Burgner-Kahrs, Timothy D. Barfoot_
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  State estimation techniques for continuum robots (CRs) typically involve using computationally complex dynamic models, simplistic shape approximations, or are limited to quasi-static methods. These approaches can be sensitive to unmodeled disturbances acting on the robot. Inspired by a factor-graph optimization paradigm, this work introduces a continuous-time stochastic state estimation framework for continuum robots. We introduce factors based on continuous-time kinematics that are corrupted by a white noise Gaussian process (GP). By using a simple robot model paired with high-rate sensing, we show adaptability to unmodeled external forces and data dropout. We demonstrate our method on a CR with gyroscope and pose sensors, highlighting its versatility in real-world systems.

- **[From Emergence to Reversibility](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09054)**  `arXiv:2611.09054`  `cs.MA`  
  _Zhengye Han, Linda Ng Boyle_
  <details open><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Multi-agent systems become capable through institutional mechanisms, such as rules that route information to agents that act on it. These mechanisms are also natural places to restrict the system. Yet as agents learn, share memory, delegate, and repair failures, the collective can find other paths to the same effects. A restriction on the original mechanism can then keep operating but no longer stops them, while routine performance and compliance still passes. We propose \textbf{Reversible Multi-agent Systems} as a research agenda: collectives that grow while an authorized supervisor can still restrict specified effects, keep them restricted as agents adapt, and reconstitute useful cooperation under renewed authority. We outline how to evaluate control across growth paths, back control claims with evidence, and allocate the rights to restrict and restore.
